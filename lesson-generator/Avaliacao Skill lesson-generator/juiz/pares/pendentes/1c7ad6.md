# Exothermic batch reactor with a cooling jacket

[problem] 

An exothermic liquid-phase reaction A → B, first order in A, takes place in a jacketed batch reactor. The rate constant is k = k0·exp(−E/(RT)), with k0 = 6.0×10⁶ s⁻¹, E = 60 kJ/mol and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). The initial concentration of A is CA(0) = 2000 mol/m³, the heat of reaction is ΔH = −80 kJ/mol and the volumetric heat capacity of the mixture is ρcp = 4.0×10⁶ J/(m³·K). The jacket stays at Tj = 300 K and removes heat with UA/V = 2000 W/(m³·K). The reactor starts at T(0) = 320 K.

What is the maximum temperature of the reactor, in K?

At what time does the maximum occur, in min?

What is the conversion of A after 30 min, in %?

How long does it take to reach 95 % conversion, in min?

The reaction releases heat and the jacket removes it. The heat warms the liquid, which speeds the reaction up; meanwhile A is consumed and the rate eventually falls. At the end you will have a Python program that follows concentration and temperature over time and answers the four questions.

## Model

### Mass balance of component A, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of A = in − out − consumption⟧ 

(principle) A does not appear or disappear for no reason. The control volume is the liquid inside the reactor.

 ⟦(d ( V ⋅ C_A ))/(d t) {accumulation} = 0 {nothing enters} − 0 {nothing leaves} − r ⋅ V {consumption}⟧ 

CA — concentration of reactant A inside the reactor, mol/m³

V — volume of liquid in the reactor, m³

r — reaction rate per volume of liquid, mol/(m³·s)

(translation) A batch reactor has no feed and no outlet, so both stream terms are zero. Each remaining word becomes a molar flow of A, in mol/s.

 ⟦r = k ⋅ C_A⟧ 

k — rate constant of the reaction, which depends on the temperature as in the Arrhenius law below, 1/s

(kinetics) First order in A, as the problem states.

 ⟦V = constant⟧ 

(assumption) A liquid-phase reaction changes the density very little. If the volume changed noticeably, V would stay inside the derivative and would need a balance of its own.

[final] 

 ⟦(d C_A )/(d t) {accumulation of A} = − k ⋅ C_A {consumption}⟧ 

(algebra) Everything divided by V, which is constant.

The concentration of reactant A only falls, at a speed proportional to itself. The catch is that k depends on the temperature, so this equation cannot be solved alone: it needs the energy balance.

### Energy balance of the liquid, in joules

 ⟦accumulation of energy = heat released by the reaction − heat removed by the jacket⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. The control volume is the same liquid; with no streams, energy only changes through the reaction and the exchange with the jacket.

 ⟦ρ c_p ⋅ V ⋅ (d T)/(d t) {accumulation of energy} = ( − Δ H ) ⋅ r ⋅ V {heat of reaction} − U A ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {exchange with the jacket}⟧ 

T — temperature of the liquid in the reactor, K

ρcp — volumetric heat capacity of the mixture, the energy needed to warm 1 m³ by 1 K, J/(m³·K)

ΔH — heat of reaction per mole of A reacted, negative because the reaction is exothermic, J/mol

UA — overall heat transfer coefficient times jacket area, W/K

Tj — temperature of the jacket, K

(translation) The reaction releases (−ΔH) joules per mole, at r·V moles per second. The jacket takes heat away when the liquid is hotter than it, and would bring heat in if it were colder.

 ⟦ρ c_p = constant , T_j = constant⟧ 

(assumption) The mixture keeps the same heat capacity as A turns into B, and the jacket fluid is so well supplied that its temperature does not move. If the jacket warmed up, it would need an energy balance of its own and would remove less heat.

[final] 

 ⟦(d T)/(d t) {heating rate} = ( ( − ΔH ) ⋅ k ⋅ C_A {heat released} − (U A)/(V) ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {heat removed} )/(ρ c_p )⟧ 

(algebra) Everything divided by V, with r = kCA; this gives the form with UA/V used in the problem.

The temperature rises while the heat released exceeds the heat removed and falls when the order reverses. The maximum is the instant when the two are equal, and its value depends on how much A is left at that moment.

[eq-block] Arrhenius law: 

 ⟦k = k_0 ⋅ exp ( − (E)/(R ⋅ T) )⟧ 

k0 — pre-exponential factor, 1/s

E — activation energy, J/mol

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Given by the problem. It couples the two balances: the temperature sets k, and k sets how fast heat is released. The temperature must be in kelvin.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Numerical integration

The unknowns CA and T change with time and each derivative depends on both: a system of ordinary differential equations with no formula, because k depends on the temperature. To solve this kind of problem, we will use numerical integration: starting from the initial values, the computer advances time in many small steps, using the derivatives to estimate the next values, and chooses the step size by itself to keep the error small. It fits because we know the starting state and the rules of change. In Python, the function that does this is solve_ivp, from the library scipy.integrate. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Balance function

[purpose] Write the two derivatives of the model in a function, the only piece of the program that carries the engineering.

```
# --- Balances: derivatives of the state y = [CA, T] ---
def balances(t, y, UA_V, Tj):
 CA, T = y
 k = k0 * np.exp(-E / (R * T)) # 1/s
 r = k * CA # mol/(m3 s)
 dCA = -r # mol/(m3 s)
 dT = ((-dH) * r - UA_V * (T - Tj)) / rho_cp # K/s
 return [dCA, dT]
```

[how] def balances(t, y, UA_V, Tj): defines a function, a named block of code that receives values and returns a result. The integrator will call it many times, each time with the current time t in s, the current state y and the two extra values UA_V and Tj, and will use what it returns to decide the next step. The state always arrives as a list; CA, T = y is unpacking: the first element goes to CA, the second to T. np.exp is the exponential. The last line, return [dCA, dT], gives back a list with the two derivatives, in the same order as the state. The -dH turns the negative heat of reaction into heat released. The time t is not used because the equations do not depend on it explicitly, but the integrator always passes it first.

[so-far] Given a state, the program can now compute how fast it changes.

### Event and integration

[purpose] Ask the integrator to advance from 0 to 2 h and to note when the concentration of A drops to 5 % of its initial value, which is 95 % conversion.

```
# --- Event: CA falls to 5 % of the initial value ---
def reach95(t, y, UA_V, Tj):
 return y[0] - 0.05 * CA0
# --- Integration over 2 h ---
sol = solve_ivp(balances, [0, 7200], [CA0, T0], args=(UA_V, Tj),
 events=reach95, dense_output=True,
 rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-8)
```

[how] The function reach95 is an event function: the integrator calls it with the same arguments as balances and records the instant at which the value it returns crosses zero. Here it returns y[0] - 0.05 * CA0; y[0] is the first element of the state (Python counts from 0), the concentration of A, so the result is zero at 95 % conversion. The call solve_ivp receives:

[how] 

balances — the function written before, passed without parentheses: we hand over the function itself and solve_ivp calls it.

[0, 7200] — initial and final times, s.

[CA0, T0] — initial state, in the same order as the derivatives.

args=(UA_V, Tj) — extra values that solve_ivp appends to every call of balances and reach95. It is a tuple (a fixed list in parentheses); the comma matters when there is a single item.

events=reach95 — the event function to watch.

dense_output=True — keeps a continuous solution, so we can ask for the state at any instant afterwards.

rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-8 — tolerances: the largest relative and absolute error accepted at each step. The integrator shrinks the step until its estimated error is below them. Tightening them until the first decimal of the answers stopped changing is the precision criterion.

### Why does the temperature rise first and then fall?

At the start the temperature climbs from 320 K because the heat released by the reaction exceeds what the jacket removes. The peak, 341.3 K at 13.7 min, is the instant when the two are equal. After that, reactant A is so depleted that the heat released drops below the heat removed, and the liquid cools toward the jacket at 300 K.

### Why is the conversion already 95 % at 18.0 min, after the peak?

The reactant A concentration falls fastest while the temperature is high, around the peak at 13.7 min. By the time the liquid cools, little A is left; 99.2 % has reacted at 30 min.

A stronger jacket removes heat faster. The temperature peak becomes much smaller and arrives earlier, and as the liquid stays cooler the reaction is slower, so reaching a given conversion takes longer.

## Model

### Mass balance of component A, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of A = in − out − consumption⟧ 

(principle) Moles of A are conserved except where the reaction destroys them. The control volume is the liquid inside the reactor.

 ⟦(d ( V ⋅ C_A ))/(d t) {accumulation} = 0 {in} − 0 {out} − r ⋅ V {consumption}⟧ 

CA — concentration of A in the liquid of the reactor, mol/m³

V — volume of liquid in the reactor, m³

r — rate of the reaction, moles of A consumed per m³ of liquid per second, mol/(m³·s)

t — time since the start of the batch, s

(translation) Each word becomes a molar flow of A, in mol/s. A batch reactor has no feed and no outlet, so "in" and "out" are zero.

 ⟦r = k ( T ) ⋅ C_A⟧ 

k — rate constant, which depends on the temperature of the liquid, s⁻¹

T — temperature of the liquid in the reactor, K

(kinetics) First order in A, as the problem states. The temperature enters through k, and that is what ties this balance to the energy balance.

 ⟦V = constant⟧ 

(assumption) The liquid barely changes density as A turns into B, so its volume stays put. If it did change, V would have to stay inside the derivative and be computed from the composition at each instant.

[final] 

 ⟦(d C_A )/(d t) {change of A} = − k ( T ) ⋅ C_A {consumption by reaction}⟧ 

(algebra) Kinetics substituted and everything divided by the constant V.

A disappears at a rate proportional to how much A is left. If T were fixed, this would be a plain exponential decay; because T changes, k changes during the batch, and the equation cannot be solved without knowing T(t) at the same time.

[eq-block] Arrhenius law: 

 ⟦k ( T ) = k_0 ⋅ exp ( − (E)/(R ⋅ T) )⟧ 

k0 — pre-exponential factor, 6.0×10⁶ s⁻¹

E — activation energy, 60 000 J/mol

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Given in the statement. With an activation energy this large, a few kelvin more make k noticeably larger: this strong sensitivity is what lets the heat of reaction feed on itself.

[eq-block] Conversion: 

 ⟦X = 1 − (C_A )/(C_A 0 )⟧ 

X — conversion of A, the fraction of the initial A that has already reacted, dimensionless (shown in %)

CA0 — concentration of A at the start of the batch, mol/m³

The definition the questions use: 95 % conversion means CA = 0.05·2000 = 100 mol/m³.

### Energy balance of the reactor contents, in watts

 ⟦accumulation of energy = heat released by reaction − heat removed by the jacket⟧ 

(principle) Conservation of energy for the same control volume, the liquid in the reactor. With no streams, no energy enters or leaves with matter; stirring work is neglected next to the heat of reaction.

 ⟦(d ( ρ c_p ⋅ V ⋅ T ))/(d t) {accumulation of energy} = ( − Δ H_r ) ⋅ r ⋅ V {heat of reaction} − U A ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {exchange with the jacket}⟧ 

ρcp — volumetric heat capacity of the mixture, the energy to heat 1 m³ of liquid by 1 K, J/(m³·K)

ΔHr — heat of reaction per mole of A reacted, negative because the reaction is exothermic, J/mol

UA — product of the overall heat transfer coefficient and the jacket area, W/K

Tj — temperature of the jacket fluid, K

(translation) Each word becomes a power, in W. The minus sign in (−ΔHr) turns the negative heat of reaction into a positive heat release.

 ⟦ρ c_p , V = constant⟧ 

(assumption) The heat capacity does not change with composition or temperature over the temperature range of this batch, and the volume is constant as in the mass balance. If ρcp varied, it would have to be evaluated at each instant and the energy balance written in enthalpy.

[final] 

 ⟦(d T)/(d t) {heating rate} = ( ( − Δ H_r ) ⋅ k ( T ) ⋅ C_A {generated, per m³} − (U A)/(V) ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {removed, per m³} )/(ρ c_p )⟧ 

(algebra) Constants taken out of the derivative, kinetics substituted, and everything divided by ρcp·V. The statement gives UA/V directly, so V itself is never needed.

The temperature rises while the heat generated per m³ exceeds the heat removed per m³ and falls when the jacket wins. The maximum temperature is exactly the instant when the two are equal, dT/dt = 0. Since the generated term contains CA, it shrinks as A runs out, so the jacket always wins in the end.

### Balances as a Python function

[purpose] Turn the two model equations into a function that, given the time and the state, returns dCA/dt and dT/dt. This is what the integrator will call.

```
def balances(t, y, UAV):
 CA, T = y # unpack the state
 k = k0 * np.exp(-E / (R * T)) # rate constant, 1/s
 r = k * CA # reaction rate, mol/(m³·s)
 dCA = -r
 dT = (-dH * r - UAV * (T - Tj)) / rho_cp
 return [dCA, dT]
```

[how] def creates a function: a named recipe that receives values (its arguments, in parentheses) and gives back a result with return. The indented lines are its body. The arguments are fixed by solve_ivp: first the time t, then the state y, which always arrives as a list (here, two numbers: CA and T, in that order), then any extra values; UAV is passed as an extra so the check can rerun the same function with no jacket. CA, T = y is unpacking: it gives a name to each item of the list, in order. np.exp is the exponential. The return is a list with the two derivatives in the same order as the state. t is not used in the body, because nothing in the model depends explicitly on the clock, but it must be there because the integrator always sends it first.

[so-far] The program can evaluate the right-hand side of the model for any state.

### Events: the instants the questions ask for

[purpose] Two of the answers are instants: when T stops rising and when X reaches 95 %. Instead of searching for them afterwards in a table of points, we let the integrator detect them during the integration.

```
def peak(t, y, UAV):
 return balances(t, y, UAV)[1] # dT/dt: zero at the peak
peak.direction = -1 # only when dT/dt goes + to −

def x95(t, y, UAV):
 return y[0] - 0.05 * CA0 # zero when X = 95 %
```

[how] An event is a function with the same arguments as balances that returns a number; solve_ivp watches its sign and, when it changes between two steps, finds the exact instant where it crosses zero. peak returns dT/dt: balances(...) gives the list of derivatives, and [1] takes its second item (Python counts from 0, so [0] is the first). peak.direction = -1 sets an attribute of the function, a label that solve_ivp reads: only crossings from positive to negative count, that is, a maximum and not a minimum. x95 returns CA − 100 mol/m³, which is zero exactly at 95 % conversion.

### Integration in time

[purpose] March the model from the initial state to 60 min, catching both events on the way.

```
sol = solve_ivp(balances, (0, t_end), [CA0, T0],
 args=(UAV,), events=[peak, x95],
 rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-6, dense_output=True)
```

[how] solve_ivp starts at t = 0 with the initial state, calls balances to get the derivatives, takes a time step, and repeats, a few hundred times in this case. At each call it passes the current time, the current state as a list, and then the contents of args. It picks the step size itself: short when the state changes fast (around the peak), long when it is calm. Its arguments:

[how] 

balances — the function itself, without parentheses: we hand over the recipe, and solve_ivp decides when to call it.

(0, t_end) — the time interval, from 0 to 3600 s.

[CA0, T0] — the initial state, in the same order the function unpacks: 2000 mol/m³ and 320 K.

args=(UAV,) — extra values added to every call, after t and y; here, the jacket coefficient. It must be a tuple (a fixed list written with parentheses), and the comma is what makes (UAV,) a tuple of one item instead of just a number in parentheses.

events=[peak, x95] — the functions to watch, from the previous step.

rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-6 — the tolerances: the error the integrator accepts at each step, relative to the size of each variable (one part in 10⁸) and absolute (10⁻⁶, in the units of the state). It shortens the step until its own error estimate is below them. They are this tight so that the printed decimals would not change with tighter values.

dense_output=True — keeps an interpolating formula between steps, so the solution can be read at any instant, such as exactly 30 min.

The result sol is an object that bundles everything: sol.t_events holds the instants of each event, sol.y_events the state at each of them, and sol.sol(t) evaluates the state at any time t.

### Complete program

```
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Data of the problem (SI units)
k0 = 6.0e6 # pre-exponential factor, 1/s
E = 60e3 # activation energy, J/mol
R = 8.314 # gas constant, J/(mol·K)
CA0 = 2000.0 # initial concentration of A, mol/m³
dH = -80e3 # heat of reaction, J/mol
rho_cp = 4.0e6 # volumetric heat capacity, J/(m³·K)
Tj = 300.0 # jacket temperature, K
UAV = 2000.0 # U·A/V, W/(m³·K)
T0 = 320.0 # initial temperature, K
t_end = 3600.0 # simulated time, s (60 min)

def balances(t, y, UAV):
 CA, T = y # unpack the state
 k = k0 * np.exp(-E / (R * T)) # rate constant, 1/s
 r = k * CA # reaction rate, mol/(m³·s)
 dCA = -r
 dT = (-dH * r - UAV * (T - Tj)) / rho_cp
 return [dCA, dT]

def peak(t, y, UAV):
 return balances(t, y, UAV)[1] # dT/dt: zero at the peak
peak.direction = -1 # only when dT/dt goes + to −

def x95(t, y, UAV):
 return y[0] - 0.05 * CA0 # zero when X = 95 %

sol = solve_ivp(balances, (0, t_end), [CA0, T0],
 args=(UAV,), events=[peak, x95],
 rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-6, dense_output=True)

t_peak = sol.t_events[0][0] # s
CA_pk, T_max = sol.y_events[0][0] # state at the peak
t_95 = sol.t_events[1][0] # s
CA_30 = sol.sol(30 * 60)[0] # mol/m³ at 30 min
X_30 = 1 - CA_30 / CA0
X_pk = 1 - CA_pk / CA0

print(f"Maximum temperature: {T_max:.1f} K")
print(f"Time of the maximum: {t_peak/60:.1f} min")
print(f"Conversion after 30 min: {100*X_30:.1f} %")
print(f"Time to 95 % conversion: {t_95/60:.1f} min")
print(f"Conversion at the peak: {100*X_pk:.1f} %")

# Check: with no jacket (UAV = 0), energy and mass balances
# combine into T = T0 + dT_ad * X, with no integral at all
dT_ad = -dH * CA0 / rho_cp # adiabatic rise, K
ad = solve_ivp(balances, (0, t_end), [CA0, T0], args=(0.0,),
 t_eval=[600], rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-6)
CA_ad, T_ad = ad.y[:, 0] # state at 10 min
X_ad = 1 - CA_ad / CA0
print(f"Adiabatic rise: {dT_ad:.1f} K")
print(f"Check, no jacket, at 10 min: X = {100*X_ad:.1f} %")
print(f" integrated T = {T_ad:.2f} K")
print(f" T0 + dT_ad*X = {T0 + dT_ad*X_ad:.2f} K")

# Figure: conversion and temperature against time
t = np.linspace(0, t_end, 601) # s
CA, T = sol.sol(t)
fig, ax1 = plt.subplots()
ax1.plot(t/60, 100*(1 - CA/CA0), color="tab:blue",
 label="Conversion of A")
ax1.set_xlabel("Time (min)")
ax1.set_ylabel("Conversion of A (%)")
ax2 = ax1.twinx()
ax2.plot(t/60, T, color="tab:red", label="Temperature")
ax2.set_ylabel("Temperature (K)")
fig.legend(loc="center right")
plt.show()
```

### Why does the temperature rise at first, even with a jacket 20 K colder?

At the start, the heat removed per m³ is 2000·(320 − 300) = 40 000 W/m³, while the reaction, with all 2000 mol/m³ of A still present, releases more than that. The surplus heats the liquid, the warmer liquid reacts faster, and the Temperature curve steepens over the first minutes instead of flattening.

### What stops the temperature at 341.3 K?

Not the jacket getting stronger, but A running out: at the peak, at 13.7 min, the Conversion of A is already 86.9 %, so only 100 − 86.9 = 13.1 % of the reactant is left to release heat. From there, the heat generated per m³ falls below the heat removed and the Temperature slides back toward 300 K over the rest of the hour.

### Why does conversion go from 95 % to 99.2 % so slowly?

The Conversion of A passes 95 % at 18.0 min, during the hot stretch just after the peak; the last 99.2 − 95 = 4.2 percentage points take the next 30 − 18 = 12 min, because the reaction is now first order on a small CA and, at the same time, the cooling Temperature makes k smaller.

### Lower UA/V to 1000 W/(m³·K)

With half the cooling, the self-heating has more room: the Temperature peak climbs and the Conversion of A curve becomes almost a step. The peak moves very little in time, because the faster reaction also exhausts A sooner.
