# Adiabatic flame temperature of methane

[problem] 

Methane is burned completely, CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, with 20% excess dry air (21% O2 and 79% N2, molar) in an adiabatic combustor. Fuel and air enter at T0 = 298.15 K. The heat of combustion at 298.15 K, with water as vapor, is ΔH°c = −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4. The ideal-gas heat capacities follow Cp/R = A + B·T + D/T² (T in K, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)):

CO2: A = 5.457, B = 1.045×10⁻³, D = −1.157×10⁵

H2O: A = 3.470, B = 1.450×10⁻³, D = 0.121×10⁵

N2: A = 3.280, B = 0.593×10⁻³, D = 0.040×10⁵

O2: A = 3.639, B = 0.506×10⁻³, D = −0.227×10⁵

Questions:

Calculate the adiabatic flame temperature with 20% excess air (K).

What would it be with 100% excess air (K)?

What percentage of excess air gives a flame temperature of 1500 K?

All the heat the reaction releases stays in the gases that leave the combustor: the products and the air that did not react. The more excess air, the more gas shares the same heat, and the cooler the flame. At the end you will have a program that finds the flame temperature for any excess air, and the excess air for any flame temperature.

## Model

### Mass balance of each species, in moles per mol of CH4

 ⟦out = in + generation by the reaction⟧ 

(principle) Each species is conserved except for what the reaction makes or consumes. The control volume is the combustor, at steady state, so nothing accumulates; the basis is 1 mol of CH4 fed.

 ⟦n_i {leaves} = n_i , 0 {enters} + ν_i ⋅ ξ {made by the reaction}⟧ 

ni — moles of species i that leave the combustor per mol of CH4 fed, mol

ni,0 — moles of species i that enter per mol of CH4 fed, mol

νi — stoichiometric coefficient of i in CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O: −1 for CH4, −2 for O2, +1 for CO2, +2 for H2O, 0 for N2

ξ — extent of reaction, the moles of CH4 that react per mol of CH4 fed, mol

(translation) Each word becomes a number of moles of species i.

 ⟦ξ = 1⟧ 

(assumption) Combustion is complete: all the CH4 becomes CO2 and H2O. If it were not, unburned CH4 or CO would leave, less heat would be released, and the flame would be cooler.

 ⟦n_O_2 , 0 = 2 ( 1 + e ) n_N_2 , 0 = (79)/(21) ⋅ 2 ( 1 + e )⟧ 

e — fraction of excess air, the O2 fed beyond the 2 mol the reaction needs, divided by those 2 mol: 0.20 for 20% excess, dimensionless

(definition) Excess air is measured against the stoichiometric O2, 2 mol per mol of CH4. Each mol of O2 brings 79/21 = 3.762 mol of N2, the molar ratio in air.

[final] 

 ⟦n_CO_2 = 1 {from the carbon} n_H_2 O = 2 {from the hydrogen} n_O_2 = 2 e {oxygen not used} n_N_2 = (79)/(21) ⋅ 2 ( 1 + e ) {inert, as it entered}⟧ 

(algebra) The balance of each species with ξ = 1 and the feed above; CH4 leaves with zero moles.

This is the composition of the gas that leaves the combustor. Only the O2 and N2 depend on the excess air, and both grow with it: excess air adds gas that must be heated without adding any heat.

### Overall energy balance of the combustor, in J per mol of CH4

 ⟦energy out with the stream = energy in with the stream + heat − shaft work⟧ 

(principle) First law for an open system at steady state. The control volume is the combustor, with fuel and air entering and the hot gases leaving.

 ⟦H_out ( T ) {enthalpy of the products} = H_in ( T_0 ) {enthalpy of fuel and air} + Q {heat} − W_s {shaft work}⟧ 

T — adiabatic flame temperature, the temperature of the gas leaving the combustor, K

Hout, Hin — enthalpy of the stream leaving and of the streams entering, per mol of CH4, J

T0 — temperature at which fuel and air enter, 298.15 K

Q, Ws — heat received and shaft work done, per mol of CH4, J

(translation) The energy carried by a stream is its enthalpy; changes of kinetic and potential energy are negligible next to a heat of combustion.

 ⟦Q = 0 W_s = 0 ⇒ H_out ( T ) − H_in ( T_0 ) = 0⟧ 

(assumption) The combustor is adiabatic and has no moving shaft. If it lost heat to the walls, the gas would leave cooler than the adiabatic flame temperature, which is therefore the highest the gas can reach.

 ⟦Δ H_c^(°) {react at T₀} + ∑ {i} n_i ∫_T_0^(T) C_p , i d T {heat the products from T₀ to T} = 0⟧ 

ΔH°c — heat of combustion at T0, water as vapor, −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4

Cp,i — ideal-gas heat capacity of product i, J/(mol·K)

(algebra) Enthalpy is a state function, so the change from inlet to outlet can follow any path. The convenient one: react at T0, where the heat of combustion is known, then heat the products from T0 to T.

 ⟦∫_T_0^(T) (C_p , i )/(R) d T = A_i ( T − T_0 ) + (B_i )/(2) ( T^(2) − T_0^(2) ) − D_i ( (1)/(T) − (1)/(T_0 ) )⟧ 

(algebra) The heat capacity correlation (below) integrated term by term: the integral of D/T² is −D/T.

[final] 

 ⟦Δ H_c^(°) {heat released} + R ∑ {i} n_i [ A_i ( T − T_0 ) + (B_i )/(2) ( T^(2) − T_0^(2) ) − D_i ( (1)/(T) − (1)/(T_0 ) ) ] {heat absorbed by the outlet gas} = 0⟧ 

(algebra) The integral of the previous step, multiplied by R, placed in the balance, with the ni of the mass balance.

The heat the reaction releases is exactly the heat that warms the outlet gas from T0 to T. The unknown T appears as T, T² and 1/T at once, so there is no formula that isolates it. The excess air e, on the other hand, appears only in the ni, and in a linear way.

[eq-block] Ideal-gas heat capacity: 

 ⟦(C_p , i )/(R) = A_i + B_i ⋅ T + (D_i )/(T^(2) )⟧ 

Ai, Bi, Di — correlation constants of gas i, dimensionless, 1/K and K², given in the statement

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Given by the statement. Heat capacities grow with temperature, which is why a single average value would not be exact between T0 and the flame temperature.

[eq-block] Excess air for a chosen flame temperature: 

 ⟦e = (− Δ H_c^(°) − S_0 )/(S_1 − S_0 )⟧ 

S0 — heat absorbed by the outlet gas, the second term of the energy balance, computed with e = 0 at the chosen temperature, J per mol of CH4

S1 — the same, with e = 1, J per mol of CH4

With T fixed, the heat absorbed is a straight line in e, S0 + (S1 − S0)·e, because the moles of O2 and N2 grow linearly with e. Setting it equal to −ΔH°c gives this formula, which answers the third question directly.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Root finding

The energy balance is a single equation in a single unknown, T, that cannot be rearranged into a formula. To solve this kind of problem, we will use root finding: we write the balance as a function, the residual, that is zero at the answer, and a method shrinks an interval where it changes sign. It fits because the residual is negative at T0 and positive at 4000 K, so one root lies in between; the third question needs only the formula for e. In Python, the function that does this is brentq, from the library SciPy. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Composition of the outlet gas

### Energy balance as a residual

[purpose] Write the energy balance as a function of T that is zero at the flame temperature: the residual that the root finder will drive to zero.

```
def heat_up(T, n):
 """Enthalpy to heat the products n from T0 to T, J/mol CH4."""
 total = 0.0
 for gas, moles in n.items():
 A, B, D = cp[gas]
 integral = (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))
 total += moles * R * integral
 return total

def residual(T, e):
 """Energy balance: zero at the adiabatic flame temperature."""
 return dHc + heat_up(T, products(e))
```

[how] heat_up receives a temperature T, in K, and a composition n (the dictionary from products), and returns the second term of the energy balance. for gas, moles in n.items(): is a loop: n.items() gives the pairs (name, moles) one at a time, and the indented lines run once per gas, with gas and moles taking the values of that pair. A, B, D = cp[gas] is unpacking: the three numbers of the tuple go into three names at once. T**2 is T squared. total += … adds to total the heat absorbed by this gas; the expression is split over two lines, which Python accepts inside parentheses. residual is the energy balance: heat of combustion (negative) plus heat absorbed (positive). It receives T first, because the root finder will vary this argument, and the excess air e second.

[so-far] For a guessed temperature, the program says how far the energy balance is from closing: negative if the guess is too cold, positive if too hot.

### How much does going from 20% to 100% excess air cost?

The flame temperature falls from 2066.4 K to 1486.4 K, that is, 2066.4 − 1486.4 = 580.0 K. The heat released is the same 802.6 kJ in both cases; what changes is the amount of gas sharing it, since every extra mol of O2 brings 79/21 mol of N2 with it.

Up to 100% the flame temperature loses 580.0 K; from 100% to 200% it goes from 1486.4 K to 1140.3 K. Each new mol of air is a smaller fraction of a gas stream that is already large, so it dilutes the heat less. Also, the colder the gas, the smaller its heat capacity, which slows the fall further.

## Model

### Mass balance of component CH4, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of CH4 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) CH4 does not appear or disappear for no reason. The control volume is the combustor; at steady state the accumulation is zero.

 ⟦0 = n_CH 4 , in {in} − n_CH 4 , out {out} + ν_CH 4 ⋅ ξ {generation}⟧ 

nCH4,in, nCH4,out — moles of CH4 entering and leaving the combustor, mol

νCH4 — stoichiometric coefficient of CH4: −1, because it is consumed

ξ — extent of reaction, mol of reaction per mol of CH4 fed

(translation) Each word becomes a molar flow. The reaction is CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, so the coefficients are −1, −2, +1 and +2 for CH4, O2, CO2 and H2O.

 ⟦n_CH 4 , out = 0⟧ 

(assumption) Complete combustion: no CH4 leaves. If some CH4 escaped, the outlet would contain it and the extent of reaction would be smaller than 1.

 ⟦n_CH 4 , in = 1 mol⟧ 

(definition) Basis of the calculation: 1 mol of CH4 fed. All the amounts in this model are per mol of CH4.

[final] 

 ⟦ξ = 1 mol of reaction per mol of CH4⟧ 

(algebra) Substituting the assumption and the basis into the balance: 0 = 1 − 0 − ξ.

Each mole of CH4 makes one reaction, so all the carbon ends up as CO2 and all the hydrogen as H2O. The other balances use this extent.

### Mass balance of component O2, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of O2 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) O2 comes with the air and is consumed by the reaction. The control volume is the combustor, at steady state.

 ⟦0 = n_O 2 , in {in} − n_O 2 , out {out} + ν_O 2 ⋅ ξ {generation}⟧ 

nO2,in, nO2,out — moles of O2 entering with the air and leaving in the products, mol per mol of CH4

νO2 — stoichiometric coefficient of O2: −2

(translation) The same form as for CH4, with the coefficient of O2.

 ⟦n_O 2 , in = 2 ( 1 + e )⟧ 

e — excess of air as a fraction: 0.20 for 20%, 1.00 for 100%

(definition) The reaction needs 2 mol of O2 per mol of CH4; the excess supplies the same amount, multiplied by e.

[final] 

 ⟦n_O 2 , out = 2 e⟧ 

(algebra) Substituting ξ = 1 and the definition above: 0 = 2(1 + e) − nO2,out − 2.

The excess oxygen leaves unburned. At 20% excess it is 2 × 0.20 = 0.40 mol per mol of CH4.

### Mass balance of component N2, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of N2 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) N2 is carried by the air and does not react, so it leaves in the products.

 ⟦0 = n_N 2 , in − n_N 2 , out⟧ 

(translation) With no generation, the balance is in minus out.

[final] 

 ⟦n_N 2 , out = (0.79)/(0.21) ⋅ 2 ( 1 + e )⟧ 

(definition) The air has 79 mol of N2 for every 21 mol of O2, so the N2 is the O2 entering multiplied by 0.79/0.21.

### Mass balance of component CO2, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of CO2 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) CO2 is not in the feed; it is formed by the reaction and leaves in the products.

 ⟦0 = 0 − n_CO 2 , out + 1 ⋅ ξ⟧ 

(translation) The coefficient of CO2 is +1, so the generation is the extent.

[final] 

 ⟦n_CO 2 , out = 1⟧ 

(algebra) With ξ = 1, one mol of CO2 leaves per mol of CH4.

### Mass balance of component H2O, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of H2O = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) H2O is not in the feed; it is formed by the reaction and leaves in the products.

 ⟦0 = 0 − n_H 2 O , out + 2 ⋅ ξ⟧ 

(translation) The coefficient of H2O is +2, so the generation is twice the extent.

[final] 

 ⟦n_H 2 O , out = 2⟧ 

(algebra) With ξ = 1, two mol of H2O leave per mol of CH4.

### Overall energy balance of the combustor, in joules

 ⟦accumulation of energy = energy in − energy out + heat in − work out⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. The control volume is the combustor; at steady state the accumulation is zero.

 ⟦0 = H_in {energy in} − H_out {energy out} + Q {heat in} − W {work out}⟧ 

Hin, Hout — enthalpy of the feed at 298.15 K and of the products at T, J

Q, W — heat entering and shaft work leaving the combustor, J

(translation) Each term is an energy flow carried by the streams, plus heat and work at the walls and shaft.

 ⟦Q = 0 , W = 0⟧ 

(assumption) The combustor is adiabatic and has no shaft work, and the kinetic and potential energies of the streams are negligible. If heat were lost through the walls, the flame would be colder than computed here.

 ⟦H_out − H_in = ΔH_c + ∑ {i} n_i , out ⋅ ∫ C_p , i d T {heating the products}⟧ 

(definition) Path: the reactants burn at 298.15 K, releasing ΔHc, and then the products are heated from 298.15 K to T. The reactants are already at 298.15 K, so this path gives the same change of enthalpy.

Here ΔHc is the heat of combustion, −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4, and the sum is over the products.

 ⟦h_i ( T ) = ∫ C_p , i d T⟧ 

hi — heat to warm 1 mol of species i from 298.15 K to T, J/mol; the integral is evaluated with the correlation given below

(definition) The sensible enthalpy of each species, which the next step evaluates.

[final] 

 ⟦∑ {i} n_i , out ⋅ h_i ( T ) {heat that warms the products} = − ΔH_c {heat released}⟧ 

(algebra) With Q = W = 0 and the path above, the heat that warms the products equals the heat released.

The products take up, as sensible heat, all the energy the combustion releases. Because each species' heat grows with T at a different rate, the value of T that balances the equation is the adiabatic flame temperature. It is the only unknown.

[eq-block] Heat capacity of a gas: 

 ⟦C_p / R = A + B ⋅ T + (D)/(T^(2) )⟧ 

A, B, D — coefficients of each gas, in the table below

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

T — temperature of the gas, K

The correlation is given. The integral of Cp from 298.15 K to T is R times [A(T − T0) + B/2 (T² − T0²) − D(1/T − 1/T0)], so hi is that expression with the coefficients of species i.

Parameter | Value | Unit | Source | 

ΔHc | −802.6 | kJ per mol CH4 | givenheat of combustion, water as vapor | 

T0 | 298.15 | K | givenfuel and air inlet temperature | 

R | 8.314 | J/(mol·K) | constantgas constant | 

e | 0.20 and 1.00 | — | givenexcess of air in the first two questions; the third one is solved | 

Air | 21% O2, 79% N2 | mol | givenmolar composition of the dry air | 

A, B, D for CO2 | 5.457; 1.045×10⁻³; −1.157×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

A, B, D for H2O | 3.470; 1.450×10⁻³; 0.121×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

A, B, D for N2 | 3.280; 0.593×10⁻³; 0.040×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

A, B, D for O2 | 3.639; 0.506×10⁻³; −0.227×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

[technique] 

[technique-name] Root finding, linear solve

The flame temperature is the value of T that makes the energy balance true; T enters as a power and as 1/T, so there is no simple formula. To solve this kind of problem, we will use root finding: the program tries temperatures in an interval where the balance changes sign and narrows it until the balance is zero. The excess question is linear in the excess, so a direct formula solves it, and root finding checks it. In Python, the function that does root finding is brentq, from the library scipy.optimize. See the next section to learn how to use it.

```
}
```

[how] import loads a library: numpy for arrays, matplotlib.pyplot for the figure, brentq and quad from scipy. The name after as is a short alias. R, T0 and dHc are variables holding numbers; the comment after # is ignored by Python and gives the unit. cp is a dictionary: each gas name, such as "CO2", is a key that gives the value on its right, here a tuple of three numbers (a fixed group, written in parentheses, whose elements are read by position).

### Heat needed to warm a gas

[purpose] A function that gives the heat needed to warm one mole of a gas from 298.15 K to any temperature, and a check of it.

```
def h_sensible(gas, T):
 """Heat to warm 1 mol of gas from T0 to T, in J/mol."""
 A, B, D = cp[gas]
 return R * (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))

# Check at 1000 K: numerical integral against the formula
A, B, D = cp["N2"]
cp_N2 = lambda T: R * (A + B * T + D / T**2)
print(f"Check: {quad(cp_N2, T0, 1000)[0]:.1f} = "
 f"{h_sensible('N2', 1000):.1f} J/mol")
```

[how] def defines a function. It receives gas, the name of the species, and T, the temperature in K, and it returns the heat in J/mol. The line A, B, D = cp[gas] is unpacking: it reads the three elements of the tuple into three variables, in order. The formula is the integral of the correlation, derived in the Model section. lambda T: ... makes a short unnamed function, here the correlation itself. quad(cp_N2, T0, 1000) integrates it numerically from T0 to 1000 K; it returns a pair, and [0] takes the first element, the value of the integral. The f"…" text puts the values into the printed line, with :.1f meaning one decimal.

[so-far] The program prints the check: the numerical integral and the formula agree.

[step] 

### What the products contain

[purpose] A function that gives the moles of each product for any excess of air, and the energy residual that is zero at the flame temperature.

```
def products(excess):
 """Moles of each product per mol of CH4, for an excess of air
 (0.20 means 20%)."""
 o2_fed = 2 * (1 + excess) # 2 mol O2 to burn, plus excess
 return {
 "CO2": 1.0,
 "H2O": 2.0,
 "O2": o2_fed - 2, # the excess O2 left over
 "N2": o2_fed * 0.79 / 0.21, # the N2 that came with the air
 }

def energy_residual(T, excess):
 """Heat to warm the products from T0 to T, minus the heat
 released. It is zero at the adiabatic flame temperature."""
 n = products(excess)
 heat = sum(n[g] * h_sensible(g, T) for g in n)
 return heat + dHc
```

[how] products returns a dictionary with four entries, one per product. The number after each key is in mol per mol of CH4, and it comes from the balances: 1 mol of CO2, 2 mol of H2O, the excess O2 and the N2 that came with the air. energy_residual takes two arguments: T, the temperature to test, and excess, the excess fraction. sum(... for g in n) adds one term per key of n: for g in n visits each gas name, and n[g] reads its moles. The function returns a number in J: it is positive when the products would need more heat than the combustion gives, and negative when they would need less.

[so-far] The program knows how many moles of each product there are, and how far the energy balance is from zero at any temperature.

[step] 

### Searching for the flame temperature

[purpose] Find the temperature at which the residual is zero, for 20% and for 100% excess of air.

```
def flame_temperature(excess):
 """Temperature T at which the energy balance is zero."""
 return brentq(energy_residual, T0, 4000.0, args=(excess,))

T_20 = flame_temperature(0.20)
T_100 = flame_temperature(1.00)
print(f"Adiabatic flame temperature at 20%: {T_20:.1f} K")
print(f"Adiabatic flame temperature at 100%: {T_100:.1f} K")
```

[how] brentq(f, a, b, args=(…)) finds the root of f between a and b. Here: f is energy_residual, which brentq calls many times with different temperatures in T; a = T0 and b = 4000 K are the interval, a bracket, in which the residual must change sign (it is negative at 298.15 K, where no heat is needed, and positive at 4000 K); args=(excess,) is the path by which the excess reaches each call, as the second argument of energy_residual. The comma makes it a tuple of one element. brentq stops when the interval is narrower than its tolerance, 2×10⁻¹² K by default, far below the 0.1 K printed: the tolerance is the precision criterion of the root. It returns the temperature. flame_temperature packs that call into one name.

[so-far] The program prints the adiabatic flame temperature for 20% and for 100% excess.

[step] 

### Excess for a given temperature

[purpose] Find the excess of air that gives the target temperature, by a direct formula, and check it with the root finder.

```
T_target = 1500.0 # target flame temperature, K
n_zero = products(0.0) # products with no excess air
n_one = products(1.0) # products with 100% excess

def excess_for(T):
 """Excess of air (fraction) that gives the temperature T."""
 H0 = sum(n_zero[g] * h_sensible(g, T) for g in n_zero)
 H1 = sum((n_one[g] - n_zero[g]) * h_sensible(g, T)
 for g in n_zero)
 return (-dHc - H0) / H1

e_target = excess_for(T_target)
print(f"Excess of air for {T_target:.0f} K: {100 * e_target:.1f}%")
check = flame_temperature(e_target)
print(f"Check: adiabatic flame temperature there: {check:.1f} K")
```

[how] T_target is the temperature we want. n_zero and n_one are the products at 0% and at 100% excess. The products are linear in the excess, so the energy balance is a straight line in it: heat = H0 + e·H1, where H0 is the heat of the products with no excess and H1 is the change of that heat per unit of excess. excess_for solves the line for the excess that makes the heat equal to -dHc. The sum(...) across two lines works because the open parenthesis lets Python continue the expression. 100 * e_target converts the fraction into a percentage. The check runs the root finder on the excess just found: if the direct formula is right, the flame temperature comes out as T_target.

[so-far] The program prints the excess for 1500 K and the check, which must return 1500 K.
