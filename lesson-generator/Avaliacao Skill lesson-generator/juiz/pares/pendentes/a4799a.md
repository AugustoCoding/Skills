# Adiabatic flame temperature of methane

[problem] 

Methane (CH4) is burned completely with 20% excess dry air in an adiabatic combustor. The air is 21% O2 and 79% N2 by mole. Fuel and air enter at T0 = 298.15 K. The heat of combustion at 298.15 K, with water as vapor, is ΔHc = −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4. The ideal-gas heat capacities follow Cp/R = A + B·T + D/T², with T in K and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). The coefficients are: CO2, A = 5.457, B = 1.045×10⁻³, D = −1.157×10⁵; H2O, A = 3.470, B = 1.450×10⁻³, D = 0.121×10⁵; N2, A = 3.280, B = 0.593×10⁻³, D = 0.040×10⁵; O2, A = 3.639, B = 0.506×10⁻³, D = −0.227×10⁵.

What is the adiabatic flame temperature, in K, with 20% excess air?

What is the adiabatic flame temperature, in K, with 100% excess air?

What percentage of excess air gives an adiabatic flame temperature of 1500 K?

The heat released by the combustion warms the products, and, because no heat leaves the combustor, the products end up as hot as the chemistry allows. This is the adiabatic flame temperature. At the end, you will have a program that solves the energy balance for any excess of air and finds the excess that gives a chosen temperature.

## Model

### Mass balance of component CH4, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of CH4 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) CH4 does not appear or disappear for no reason. The control volume is the combustor; at steady state the accumulation is zero.

 ⟦0 = n_CH 4 , in {in} − n_CH 4 , out {out} + ν_CH 4 ⋅ ξ {generation}⟧ 

nCH4,in, nCH4,out — moles of CH4 entering and leaving the combustor, mol

νCH4 — stoichiometric coefficient of CH4: −1, because it is consumed

ξ — extent of reaction, mol of reaction per mol of CH4 fed

(translation) Each word becomes a molar flow. The reaction is CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, so the coefficients are −1, −2, +1 and +2 for CH4, O2, CO2 and H2O.

 ⟦n_CH 4 , out = 0⟧ 

(assumption) Complete combustion: no CH4 leaves. If some CH4 escaped, the outlet would contain it and the extent of reaction would be smaller than 1.

 ⟦n_CH 4 , in = 1 mol⟧ 

(definition) Basis of the calculation: 1 mol of CH4 fed. All the amounts in this model are per mol of CH4.

[final] 

 ⟦ξ = 1 mol of reaction per mol of CH4⟧ 

(algebra) Substituting the assumption and the basis into the balance: 0 = 1 − 0 − ξ.

Each mole of CH4 makes one reaction, so all the carbon ends up as CO2 and all the hydrogen as H2O. The other balances use this extent.

### Mass balance of component O2, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of O2 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) O2 comes with the air and is consumed by the reaction. The control volume is the combustor, at steady state.

 ⟦0 = n_O 2 , in {in} − n_O 2 , out {out} + ν_O 2 ⋅ ξ {generation}⟧ 

nO2,in, nO2,out — moles of O2 entering with the air and leaving in the products, mol per mol of CH4

νO2 — stoichiometric coefficient of O2: −2

(translation) The same form as for CH4, with the coefficient of O2.

 ⟦n_O 2 , in = 2 ( 1 + e )⟧ 

e — excess of air as a fraction: 0.20 for 20%, 1.00 for 100%

(definition) The reaction needs 2 mol of O2 per mol of CH4; the excess supplies the same amount, multiplied by e.

[final] 

 ⟦n_O 2 , out = 2 e⟧ 

(algebra) Substituting ξ = 1 and the definition above: 0 = 2(1 + e) − nO2,out − 2.

The excess oxygen leaves unburned. At 20% excess it is 2 × 0.20 = 0.40 mol per mol of CH4.

### Mass balance of component N2, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of N2 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) N2 is carried by the air and does not react, so it leaves in the products.

 ⟦0 = n_N 2 , in − n_N 2 , out⟧ 

(translation) With no generation, the balance is in minus out.

[final] 

 ⟦n_N 2 , out = (0.79)/(0.21) ⋅ 2 ( 1 + e )⟧ 

(definition) The air has 79 mol of N2 for every 21 mol of O2, so the N2 is the O2 entering multiplied by 0.79/0.21.

### Mass balance of component CO2, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of CO2 = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) CO2 is not in the feed; it is formed by the reaction and leaves in the products.

 ⟦0 = 0 − n_CO 2 , out + 1 ⋅ ξ⟧ 

(translation) The coefficient of CO2 is +1, so the generation is the extent.

[final] 

 ⟦n_CO 2 , out = 1⟧ 

(algebra) With ξ = 1, one mol of CO2 leaves per mol of CH4.

### Mass balance of component H2O, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of H2O = in − out + generation⟧ 

(principle) H2O is not in the feed; it is formed by the reaction and leaves in the products.

 ⟦0 = 0 − n_H 2 O , out + 2 ⋅ ξ⟧ 

(translation) The coefficient of H2O is +2, so the generation is twice the extent.

[final] 

 ⟦n_H 2 O , out = 2⟧ 

(algebra) With ξ = 1, two mol of H2O leave per mol of CH4.

### Overall energy balance of the combustor, in joules

 ⟦accumulation of energy = energy in − energy out + heat in − work out⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. The control volume is the combustor; at steady state the accumulation is zero.

 ⟦0 = H_in {energy in} − H_out {energy out} + Q {heat in} − W {work out}⟧ 

Hin, Hout — enthalpy of the feed at 298.15 K and of the products at T, J

Q, W — heat entering and shaft work leaving the combustor, J

(translation) Each term is an energy flow carried by the streams, plus heat and work at the walls and shaft.

 ⟦Q = 0 , W = 0⟧ 

(assumption) The combustor is adiabatic and has no shaft work, and the kinetic and potential energies of the streams are negligible. If heat were lost through the walls, the flame would be colder than computed here.

 ⟦H_out − H_in = ΔH_c + ∑ {i} n_i , out ⋅ ∫ C_p , i d T {heating the products}⟧ 

(definition) Path: the reactants burn at 298.15 K, releasing ΔHc, and then the products are heated from 298.15 K to T. The reactants are already at 298.15 K, so this path gives the same change of enthalpy.

Here ΔHc is the heat of combustion, −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4, and the sum is over the products.

 ⟦h_i ( T ) = ∫ C_p , i d T⟧ 

hi — heat to warm 1 mol of species i from 298.15 K to T, J/mol; the integral is evaluated with the correlation given below

(definition) The sensible enthalpy of each species, which the next step evaluates.

[final] 

 ⟦∑ {i} n_i , out ⋅ h_i ( T ) {heat that warms the products} = − ΔH_c {heat released}⟧ 

(algebra) With Q = W = 0 and the path above, the heat that warms the products equals the heat released.

The products take up, as sensible heat, all the energy the combustion releases. Because each species' heat grows with T at a different rate, the value of T that balances the equation is the adiabatic flame temperature. It is the only unknown.

[eq-block] Heat capacity of a gas: 

 ⟦C_p / R = A + B ⋅ T + (D)/(T^(2) )⟧ 

A, B, D — coefficients of each gas, in the table below

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

T — temperature of the gas, K

The correlation is given. The integral of Cp from 298.15 K to T is R times [A(T − T0) + B/2 (T² − T0²) − D(1/T − 1/T0)], so hi is that expression with the coefficients of species i.

Parameter | Value | Unit | Source | 

ΔHc | −802.6 | kJ per mol CH4 | givenheat of combustion, water as vapor | 

T0 | 298.15 | K | givenfuel and air inlet temperature | 

R | 8.314 | J/(mol·K) | constantgas constant | 

e | 0.20 and 1.00 | — | givenexcess of air in the first two questions; the third one is solved | 

Air | 21% O2, 79% N2 | mol | givenmolar composition of the dry air | 

A, B, D for CO2 | 5.457; 1.045×10⁻³; −1.157×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

A, B, D for H2O | 3.470; 1.450×10⁻³; 0.121×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

A, B, D for N2 | 3.280; 0.593×10⁻³; 0.040×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

A, B, D for O2 | 3.639; 0.506×10⁻³; −0.227×10⁵ | — | givenheat capacity coefficients | 

[technique] 

[technique-name] Root finding, linear solve

The flame temperature is the value of T that makes the energy balance true; T enters as a power and as 1/T, so there is no simple formula. To solve this kind of problem, we will use root finding: the program tries temperatures in an interval where the balance changes sign and narrows it until the balance is zero. The excess question is linear in the excess, so a direct formula solves it, and root finding checks it. In Python, the function that does root finding is brentq, from the library scipy.optimize. See the next section to learn how to use it.

```
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import brentq # root finding
from scipy.integrate import quad # numerical integration, for the check

R = 8.314 # gas constant, J/(mol K)
T0 = 298.15 # fuel and air inlet temperature, K
dHc = -802.6e3 # heat of combustion of CH4, J/mol (water as vapor)

cp = {
 "CO2": (5.457, 1.045e-3, -1.157e5),

 "N2": (3.280, 0.593e-3, 0.040e5),
 "O2": (3.639, 0.506e-3, -0.227e5),
}
```

R, T0 and dHc are variables holding numbers; the comment after # is ignored by Python and gives the unit. cp is a dictionary: each gas name, such as "CO2", is a key that gives the value on its right, here a tuple of three numbers (a fixed group, written in parentheses, whose elements are read by position).

### Heat needed to warm a gas

[purpose] A function that gives the heat needed to warm one mole of a gas from 298.15 K to any temperature, and a check of it.

```
def h_sensible(gas, T):
 """Heat to warm 1 mol of gas from T0 to T, in J/mol."""
 A, B, D = cp[gas]
 return R * (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))

# Check at 1000 K: numerical integral against the formula
A, B, D = cp["N2"]
cp_N2 = lambda T: R * (A + B * T + D / T**2)
print(f"Check: {quad(cp_N2, T0, 1000)[0]:.1f} = "
 f"{h_sensible('N2', 1000):.1f} J/mol")
```

[how] def defines a function. It receives gas, the name of the species, and T, the temperature in K, and it returns the heat in J/mol. The line A, B, D = cp[gas] is unpacking: it reads the three elements of the tuple into three variables, in order. The formula is the integral of the correlation, derived in the Model section. lambda T: ... makes a short unnamed function, here the correlation itself. quad(cp_N2, T0, 1000) integrates it numerically from T0 to 1000 K; it returns a pair, and [0] takes the first element, the value of the integral. The f"…" text puts the values into the printed line, with :.1f meaning one decimal.

[so-far] The program prints the check: the numerical integral and the formula agree.

### What the products contain

[purpose] A function that gives the moles of each product for any excess of air, and the energy residual that is zero at the flame temperature.

```
def products(excess):
 """Moles of each product per mol of CH4, for an excess of air
 (0.20 means 20%)."""
 o2_fed = 2 * (1 + excess) # 2 mol O2 to burn, plus excess
 return {
 "CO2": 1.0,
 "H2O": 2.0,
 "O2": o2_fed - 2, # the excess O2 left over
 "N2": o2_fed * 0.79 / 0.21, # the N2 that came with the air
 }

def energy_residual(T, excess):
 """Heat to warm the products from T0 to T, minus the heat
 released. It is zero at the adiabatic flame temperature."""
 n = products(excess)
 heat = sum(n[g] * h_sensible(g, T) for g in n)
 return heat + dHc
```

[how] products returns a dictionary with four entries, one per product. The number after each key is in mol per mol of CH4, and it comes from the balances: 1 mol of CO2, 2 mol of H2O, the excess O2 and the N2 that came with the air. energy_residual takes two arguments: T, the temperature to test, and excess, the excess fraction. sum(... for g in n) adds one term per key of n: for g in n visits each gas name, and n[g] reads its moles. The function returns a number in J: it is positive when the products would need more heat than the combustion gives, and negative when they would need less.

[so-far] The program knows how many moles of each product there are, and how far the energy balance is from zero at any temperature.

[step] 

### Searching for the flame temperature

[purpose] Find the temperature at which the residual is zero, for 20% and for 100% excess of air.

```
def flame_temperature(excess):
 """Temperature T at which the energy balance is zero."""
 return brentq(energy_residual, T0, 4000.0, args=(excess,))

T_20 = flame_temperature(0.20)
T_100 = flame_temperature(1.00)
print(f"Adiabatic flame temperature at 20%: {T_20:.1f} K")
print(f"Adiabatic flame temperature at 100%: {T_100:.1f} K")
```

[how] brentq(f, a, b, args=(…)) finds the root of f between a and b. Here: f is energy_residual, which brentq calls many times with different temperatures in T; a = T0 and b = 4000 K are the interval, a bracket, in which the residual must change sign (it is negative at 298.15 K, where no heat is needed, and positive at 4000 K); args=(excess,) is the path by which the excess reaches each call, as the second argument of energy_residual. The comma makes it a tuple of one element. brentq stops when the interval is narrower than its tolerance, 2×10⁻¹² K by default, far below the 0.1 K printed: the tolerance is the precision criterion of the root. It returns the temperature. flame_temperature packs that call into one name.

[so-far] The program prints the adiabatic flame temperature for 20% and for 100% excess.

[step] 

### Excess for a given temperature

[purpose] Find the excess of air that gives the target temperature, by a direct formula, and check it with the root finder.

```
T_target = 1500.0 # target flame temperature, K
n_zero = products(0.0) # products with no excess air
n_one = products(1.0) # products with 100% excess

def excess_for(T):
 """Excess of air (fraction) that gives the temperature T."""
 H0 = sum(n_zero[g] * h_sensible(g, T) for g in n_zero)
 H1 = sum((n_one[g] - n_zero[g]) * h_sensible(g, T)
 for g in n_zero)
 return (-dHc - H0) / H1

e_target = excess_for(T_target)
print(f"Excess of air for {T_target:.0f} K: {100 * e_target:.1f}%")
check = flame_temperature(e_target)
print(f"Check: adiabatic flame temperature there: {check:.1f} K")
```

[how] T_target is the temperature we want. n_zero and n_one are the products at 0% and at 100% excess. The products are linear in the excess, so the energy balance is a straight line in it: heat = H0 + e·H1, where H0 is the heat of the products with no excess and H1 is the change of that heat per unit of excess. excess_for solves the line for the excess that makes the heat equal to -dHc. The sum(...) across two lines works because the open parenthesis lets Python continue the expression. 100 * e_target converts the fraction into a percentage. The check runs the root finder on the excess just found: if the direct formula is right, the flame temperature comes out as T_target.

[so-far] The program prints the excess for 1500 K and the check, which must return 1500 K.

 ⟦ξ = 1 mol , n_O_2 0 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) , n_N_2 0 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) r⟧ 

ε — excess of air, as a fraction of the air needed for complete combustion, dimensionless (0.2 means 20 %)

(assumption) (definition) Complete combustion: the 1 mol of CH4 fed is entirely consumed, so ξ = 1 mol. If the combustion were partial, ξ would be smaller and CH4 and CO would appear in the products. The stoichiometric O2 is 2 mol, and the excess multiplies it by (1 + ε).

(algebra) Apply the balance of the first step to each species, with the data of the previous steps.

### Energy balance of the combustor, in joules

 ⟦enthalpy flow in = enthalpy flow out⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. For a flowing gas at steady state, with no work and with negligible kinetic and potential energy, what enters as enthalpy leaves as enthalpy. The control volume is the combustor, per mole of CH4 fed.

 ⟦0 = Δ H_c {combustion at T₀} + ∑ n_i ⋅ ΔH_i ( T ) {heating the products}⟧ 

ΔHc — heat of combustion at 298.15 K, with water as vapor, per mol of CH4, −802.6 kJ/mol

T — temperature of the gases leaving the combustor, the flame temperature, K

ΔHi — enthalpy change of 1 mol of gas i when heated from 298.15 K to T, J/mol

(translation) Enthalpy does not depend on the path, so we choose one: first the reaction takes place at 298.15 K, where the heat of combustion is known, and then the products are heated from 298.15 K to T. Fuel and air enter at 298.15 K, so the reactants need no heating term.

 ⟦ΔH_i ( T ) = R [ A_i ( T − T_0 ) + (B_i )/(2) ( T^(2) − T_0^(2) ) − D_i ( (1)/(T) − (1)/(T_0 ) ) ]⟧ 

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Ai, Bi, Di — coefficients of the heat capacity of gas i; A is dimensionless, B in 1/K and D in K²

(definition) The enthalpy change is the integral of the heat capacity, ΔHi = ∫ Cp,i dT from T₀ to T, with Cp,i/R = Ai + BiT + Di/T². Integrating term by term gives the expression above.

 ⟦Q_loss = 0⟧ 

Qloss — heat lost through the walls of the combustor, per mol of CH4, J/mol

(assumption) Adiabatic combustor, which is why the balance above has no heat term. With heat loss, a term −Qloss would appear and the flame temperature would be lower. We also treat the gases as ideal, so the enthalpy depends only on temperature and not on pressure.

[final] 

 ⟦∑ n_i ( ε ) ⋅ ΔH_i ( T ) {heat absorbed by the products} = Q = − Δ H_c {heat released by the reaction}⟧ 

Q — heat released by the combustion of 1 mol of CH4, 802 600 J/mol

(algebra) Move the heat of combustion to the other side, with Q = −ΔHc = 802.6 kJ/mol = 802 600 J/mol, and write the moles ni as functions of ε, from the mass balance.

All the heat released by the reaction ends up as sensible heat of the products: CO2, H2O, the excess O2 and the N2 of the air. The unknown is T, which appears in a polynomial and in a 1/T term, so it cannot be isolated by hand. The excess ε, on the other hand, appears linearly, and that gives a direct formula for the third question, below.

[eq-block] Excess air for a given temperature: 

 ⟦ε = (Q − ΔH_CO2 ( T ) − 2 ΔH_H2O ( T ) − 2 r ΔH_N2 ( T ))/(2 ΔH_O2 ( T ) + 2 r ΔH_N2 ( T ))⟧ 

Fixing the temperature at the target value in the final equation and putting the moles in terms of ε, the terms without ε (CO2, H2O and the N2 that accompanies the stoichiometric O2) go to the numerator, and the terms multiplied by ε (O2 and N2 in excess) to the denominator. All the enthalpy changes are evaluated at the target temperature.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Root finding

The flame temperature is the value of T that makes the energy balance true, an equation we cannot solve by hand. This kind of problem is called finding the root of a function, the point where it crosses zero. To solve it, we will use bracketing root finding (Brent's method): we give an interval where the function changes sign, and the method narrows it step by step until the zero is located with the precision wanted. It fits because the residual changes sign between 298.15 K and very high temperatures. In Python, the function that does this is brentq, from the library scipy.optimize. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Heating one mole of gas

[purpose] Turn the integral of the heat capacity into a function that gives the heat needed to take 1 mol of a gas from T₀ to T.

```
def dH(gas, T):
 """Enthalpy change of 1 mol of gas from T0 to T, J/mol."""
 A, B, D = coef[gas]
 return R * (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))
```

[how] 
def dH(gas, T): defines a function named dH. It receives the name of a gas (a text such as "CO2") and a temperature, and hands back a number with return.
The text between triple quotes is a docstring, a description of the function.
A, B, D = coef[gas] is unpacking: the tuple of three numbers found in the dictionary is split into three variables.
**2 is the square. The expression is the closed form of the integral of Cp from T0 to T, multiplied by R to get J/mol.
The line break inside the parentheses is allowed and only keeps the line short.

[so-far] The program can now compute, for any gas and any temperature, how much heat 1 mol of it absorbs when heated from 298.15 K.

[step] 

### Energy balance as a residual

[purpose] Write the balance as a function that is zero when the temperature is the right one: the heat absorbed by the products minus the heat released.

```
def moles(eps):
 """Moles leaving the combustor per mol of CH4 burned."""
 return {"CO2": 1.0, "H2O": 2.0, "O2": 2 * eps,
 "N2": 2 * (1 + eps) * r}

def residual(T, eps):
 """Heat absorbed by the products minus heat released, J."""
 n = moles(eps)
 heat = sum(n[g] * dH(g, T) for g in n)
 return heat - Q
```

[how] 
moles(eps) receives the excess of air as a fraction (0.2 for 20 %) and returns a dictionary with the moles of each gas in the products, per mole of CH4: the results of the mass balance.
residual(T, eps) receives a temperature and an excess. n[g] * dH(g, T) for g in n goes through the gases g in the dictionary n and produces, for each, moles times heat per mole; sum(…) adds them.
The function returns the heat absorbed minus Q. It is negative if T is too low (the products could still absorb more) and positive if T is too high.

[so-far] The program has a function whose zero, as a function of T, is the flame temperature.

[step] 

### Solving for the flame temperature

[purpose] Ask brentq for the temperature that zeroes the residual, for four amounts of excess air.

```
for pct in (0, 20, 100, 200):
 T = brentq(residual, T0, 5000, args=(pct / 100,))
 print(f"Excess {pct:3d} %: T = {T:.1f} K")
```

[how] brentq receives your function and searches for its zero. It calls residual many times, each time with a different trial temperature in the first argument, and uses the sign of what returns to shrink the interval. Its arguments:
[how] 
residual — the function whose zero is wanted. Do not write the parentheses: it is the function itself that is handed over, not a call.
T0, 5000 — the interval of the search, in K. The residual is negative at 298.15 K and positive at 5000 K, so a zero lies between them.
args=(pct / 100,) — the extra value that brentq passes to every call, after T. It is a tuple; the comma after the value is what makes it a tuple of one element.
[how] By default, brentq stops when the interval is narrower than about 10⁻¹² K (it has converged), far finer than the four digits we need. for pct in (0, 20, 100, 200): repeats the two indented lines once for each value of the tuple. In the f-string, {pct:3d} prints an integer (d) in a field 3 characters wide (3) and {T:.1f} a number with one decimal.

[so-far] The program prints the flame temperature for 0, 20, 100 and 200 % of excess air.

### Excess air for a target temperature, and a check

[purpose] Solve the third question directly, since the balance is linear in the excess, and confirm the result by an independent route.

```
T_target = 1500.0 # desired flame temperature, K
a = (dH("CO2", T_target) + 2 * dH("H2O", T_target)
 + 2 * r * dH("N2", T_target))
b = 2 * dH("O2", T_target) + 2 * r * dH("N2", T_target)
eps = (Q - a) / b
print(f"Excess air for {T_target:.0f} K: {100 * eps:.1f} %")

def cp(T, A, B, D):
 """Heat capacity of one gas at temperature T, J/(mol*K)."""
 return R * (A + B * T + D / T**2)

n = moles(eps)
heat = 0.0
for g in n:
 A, B, D = coef[g]
 heat += n[g] * quad(cp, T0, T_target, args=(A, B, D))[0]
print(f"Check, heat absorbed at {T_target:.0f} K: "
 f"{heat / 1000:.1f} kJ")
```

[how] 
a and b are the two pieces of the linear equation for eps at 1500 K: the heat taken by the part of the products that does not depend on the excess, and the heat taken per unit of excess. Then eps = (Q - a) / b.
cp(T, A, B, D) is the heat capacity itself, not its integral.
quad integrates a function numerically. It receives cp, the limits T0 and T_target, and args=(A, B, D), the extra values passed to cp after T. It returns a pair (value, error estimate); [0] takes the first element, the value, since the first element of a list or tuple has index 0.
heat += x adds x to heat. The loop does this for each gas.
[how] The check does not use the formula of dH: it integrates the heat capacity numerically, and the heat absorbed must equal the heat released, Q. The numerical integral stops when its estimated error is small enough, far below the 0.1 kJ printed.

[so-far] The program prints the excess air for 1500 K and shows that the products absorb exactly the heat released.

## Result

[answer] 

With 20 % excess air, the adiabatic flame temperature is 2066.4 K. With 100 % excess air it falls to 1486.4 K. A flame temperature of 1500 K requires 97.2 % excess air.

[figura; controles: Ttarget] 

### Why does the temperature fall as more air is fed?

The heat released is the same, 802.6 kJ per mol of CH4, but it is shared among more moles of gas. The flame temperature is 2311.2 K with no excess, 2066.4 K with 20 %, 1486.4 K with 100 % and 1140.3 K with 200 %.

### Where does the flame temperature reach the target?

The flame temperature crosses the target temperature of 1500 K at 97.2 % excess. At 100 % it is already 13.6 K below the target (1500 − 1486.4), which shows that the crossing is just before that point.

### Is the fall in temperature the same along the whole curve?

No. The first 20 % of excess lowers the flame temperature by 244.8 K (2311.2 − 2066.4), while the 100 points from 100 % to 200 % lower it by 346.1 K (1486.4 − 1140.3). The curve therefore flattens: the loss per percent of added air is much larger at the beginning than at the end.

### Take the target temperature to 2000 K

The target temperature line goes up and meets the flame temperature at only 26.4 % excess; at 1200 K it moves out to 177.3 %. Hotter targets need less air.
