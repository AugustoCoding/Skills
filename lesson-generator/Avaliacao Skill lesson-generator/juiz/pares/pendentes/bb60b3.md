# Dense CO₂ with the Peng–Robinson equation

[problem] 

Carbon dioxide is stored at temperature T = 320 K and pressure P = 80 bar. Use the Peng–Robinson equation of state with the critical temperature Tc = 304.13 K, the critical pressure Pc = 73.77 bar, the acentric factor ω = 0.224, the molar mass M = 44.01 g/mol and the gas constant R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).

Calculate the compressibility factor Z.

Calculate the molar volume V, in cm³/mol.

Calculate the density ρ, in kg/m³.

What molar volume Vig, in cm³/mol, would the ideal gas law give at the same conditions?

At 320 K and 80 bar the CO₂ is just above its critical point, where the molecules are close enough to attract one another strongly and the gas packs far more tightly than the ideal gas law predicts. At the end you will have a Python program that writes the Peng–Robinson equation as a cubic polynomial, finds its root, converts it into molar volume and density, checks the result by a second route and plots Z against pressure.

## Model

[eq-block] Peng–Robinson equation of state: 

 ⟦P = (R ⋅ T)/(V − b) {repulsion} − (a ( T ))/(V ( V + b ) + b ( V − b )) {attraction}⟧ 

P — absolute pressure of the stored gas, Pa

T — absolute temperature of the stored gas, K

V — molar volume of the gas, the volume occupied by one mole, m³/mol

R — gas constant, J/(mol·K)

b — co-volume, the volume that one mole of molecules itself takes up, m³/mol

a — attraction parameter, the strength of the pull between molecules at temperature T, Pa·m⁶/mol²

The first term is the ideal gas pressure, corrected for the space the molecules occupy: as V approaches b, the pressure shoots up. The second term subtracts the pressure lost because molecules pull on their neighbours instead of hitting the wall. When V is large, both corrections vanish and the equation becomes P = RT/V.

[eq-block] Peng–Robinson parameters: 

 ⟦a = 0.45724 ⋅ (R^(2) ⋅ T_c^(2) )/(P_c ) ⋅ α b = 0.07780 ⋅ (R ⋅ T_c )/(P_c ) α = [ 1 + κ ( 1 − √(T / T_c ) ) ]^(2) κ = 0.37464 + 1.54226 ⋅ ω − 0.26992 ⋅ ω^(2)⟧ 

Tc, Pc — critical temperature and critical pressure of CO₂, K and Pa

ω — acentric factor of CO₂, a measure of how far its molecules are from spherical, dimensionless

α — temperature correction of the attraction, dimensionless; equal to 1 at T = Tc

κ — slope of that correction, which depends only on ω, dimensionless

These are the fixed formulas of the Peng–Robinson model: with only Tc, Pc and ω, any substance gets its own a and b. Above Tc, α falls below 1: hotter molecules move fast enough to feel the attraction less.

[eq-block] Cubic equation in Z: 

 ⟦Z^(3) − ( 1 − B ) Z^(2) + ( A − 3 B^(2) − 2 B ) Z − ( A B − B^(2) − B^(3) ) = 0⟧ 

 ⟦Z = (P ⋅ V)/(R ⋅ T) A = (a ⋅ P)/(( R T )^(2) ) B = (b ⋅ P)/(R T)⟧ 

Z — compressibility factor of the gas: its real molar volume divided by the ideal gas molar volume at the same T and P, dimensionless

A, B — attraction and co-volume parameters made dimensionless with the conditions of the tank

Replacing V by ZRT/P in the Peng–Robinson equation and clearing the denominators turns it into this polynomial of degree three in Z. A cubic has three roots; the ones with physical meaning are real and larger than B (otherwise V < b). Above Tc, as here, only one root is real, so there is no doubt about which one to take.

[eq-block] Molar volume, density and ideal gas: 

 ⟦V = (Z ⋅ R ⋅ T)/(P) ρ = (M)/(V) V^(ig) = (R ⋅ T)/(P)⟧ 

ρ — mass density of the stored CO₂, kg/m³

M — molar mass of CO₂, kg/mol

Vig — molar volume the ideal gas law would give at the same T and P, m³/mol

The first is the definition of Z read backwards; the second divides the mass of a mole by its volume. The third is the case Z = 1, so V/Vig is exactly Z.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Polynomial roots

The problem is to find the value of Z that makes a polynomial of degree three equal to zero. To solve this kind of problem, we will use polynomial root finding: from the four coefficients, it returns all three roots at once, with no initial guess. It fits here because we must see all the roots to keep the single real one. In Python, the function that does this is roots, from the library NumPy; brentq, from SciPy, will check it. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Function for the Peng–Robinson parameters

[purpose] Turn the four parameter formulas into a function, so that a and b can be computed at any temperature, which the figure will need later.

```
def pr_params(T):
 """Peng-Robinson a(T) in Pa m6/mol2 and b in m3/mol."""
 kappa = 0.37464 + 1.54226*w - 0.26992*w**2
 alpha = (1 + kappa*(1 - np.sqrt(T/Tc)))**2
 a = 0.45724 * R**2 * Tc**2 / Pc * alpha
 b = 0.07780 * R * Tc / Pc
 return a, b

a, b = pr_params(T)
print(f"a = {a:.4f} Pa m6/mol2 b = {b*1e6:.2f} cm3/mol")
```

[how] def pr_params(T): defines a function: a named recipe that receives a temperature T and runs the indented lines below. The text in triple quotes describes it. ** is a power and np.sqrt a square root. return a, b hands back two values together, as a tuple (an ordered pair); a, b = pr_params(T) unpacks it, storing the first in a and the second in b. In the print, the f before the quotes lets values be placed between braces: {a:.4f} writes a with four decimals, and {b*1e6:.2f} writes b converted from m³/mol to cm³/mol with two decimals.

[so-far] The program computes and prints a and b for CO₂ at 320 K.

### Roots of the cubic in Z

[purpose] Build the four coefficients of the cubic at the given T and P, find all its roots and keep the real one.

```
def z_factor(T, P):
 """Largest real root of the Peng-Robinson cubic in Z."""
 a, b = pr_params(T)
 A = a * P / (R*T)**2
 B = b * P / (R*T)
 coef = [1, -(1 - B), A - 3*B**2 - 2*B, -(A*B - B**2 - B**3)]
 roots = np.roots(coef)
 real = roots[np.abs(roots.imag) < 1e-10].real
 return real.max(), A, B, roots

Z, A, B, roots = z_factor(T, P)
print(f"A = {A:.4f} B = {B:.4f}")
print("Roots of the cubic:", np.round(roots, 4))
```

[how] 

coef is a list, values between square brackets in a fixed order: the coefficients of Z³, Z², Z and the constant term, copied from the cubic of the model.

np.roots(coef) receives that single argument, the coefficients from the highest power down, and returns an array with the three roots. Some may be complex numbers, written like 0.18+0.13j, where j marks the imaginary part.

roots.imag is the imaginary part of each root and np.abs its size. np.abs(roots.imag) < 1e-10 gives True or False for each root; placed inside roots[...], it keeps only the roots marked True, that is, the real ones. The limit 10⁻¹⁰ (our choice) accepts as real a root whose imaginary part is only rounding noise. .real drops the zero imaginary part.

real.max() takes the largest real root. Here there is only one, but if a temperature below Tc gave three, the largest would be the vapor one.

The function returns four things, and the line below unpacks them into Z, A, B and roots. np.round(roots, 4) rounds the roots to four decimals for printing.

[how] The precision of np.roots is that of the computer's arithmetic, about 15 significant digits: it does not iterate toward the answer, so there is no tolerance to choose.

[so-far] The program prints A, B and the three roots, and holds the compressibility factor in Z.

### Check by a second route

[purpose] Solve the Peng–Robinson equation directly for V, in its pressure form, without the cubic. If both routes agree, the coefficients of the cubic and the choice of root are right.

```
def residual(V):
 """Peng-Robinson pressure at V minus the given pressure, Pa."""
 P_pr = R*T/(V - b) - a/(V*(V + b) + b*(V - b))
 return P_pr - P

V_check = brentq(residual, 1.01*b, 2*V_ig)
print(f"Check, P(V) solved directly: V = {V_check*1e6:.1f} cm3/mol")
```

[how] residual(V) computes the pressure the equation gives for a trial volume and subtracts the 80 bar of the tank: it is zero exactly at the answer. brentq is a root finder: it searches for where a function crosses zero, narrowing an interval at each try.

[how] 

The first argument, residual, is the function itself, without parentheses: we do not call it, we hand it over. brentq calls it many times, each time with one trial volume in V, and uses the sign of what returns to decide which half of the interval keeps the root.

1.01*b is the lower end of the interval: just above the co-volume, where the repulsion makes the pressure huge and the residual positive.

2*V_ig is the upper end: at twice the ideal volume the pressure is well below 80 bar, so the residual is negative. brentq requires opposite signs at the two ends; that is what guarantees a crossing in between.

Without a xtol argument, brentq stops when the interval that holds the root is narrower than its default tolerance, 2 × 10⁻¹² m³/mol, a millionth of a cm³/mol: far below the one decimal printed.

[so-far] The program prints a second molar volume, obtained without the cubic, to compare with the first.

### How far is the CO₂ from an ideal gas at 80 bar?

At 80 bar the Peng–Robinson Z is 0.5559 against 1 for the ideal gas Z: one mole fills 184.9 cm³ instead of 332.6 cm³, and the density is 238.0 kg/m³ instead of 132.3 kg/m³. Sizing the tank with the ideal gas law would store only a bit over half the mass you counted on. The cause is the attraction term: near the critical point the molecules pull each other together much more than the co-volume pushes them apart.

### Does the model recover the ideal gas at low pressure?

At 1 bar the program prints Z = 0.9956, practically on the ideal gas Z line. This is the second, independent check: with the molecules far apart, both corrections of the Peng–Robinson equation vanish. The first check, solving the pressure equation with brentq, gave the same 184.9 cm³/mol as the cubic.

### Why does the curve turn back up after the minimum?

The Peng–Robinson Z falls to 0.3437 at 122 bar and then rises. Squeezed further, the molecules are so close that their own volume, the b term, starts to dominate over the attraction; at still higher pressures Z would go above 1, the behaviour of a dense, nearly incompressible fluid.

### Raise the temperature T to 400 K

The dip becomes shallow and moves out of the range: Z at 80 bar rises to 0.8443, and the lowest value in the plot is 0.7132, at the edge of 200 bar. Far from the critical temperature the molecules move fast enough to feel the attraction much less, so the gas comes closer to ideal. Moving the slider toward Tc does the opposite: the drop near Pc becomes almost vertical, the signature of the critical point.

# Compressibility of carbon dioxide from the Peng-Robinson equation

[problem] 

Carbon dioxide is stored at T = 320 K and P = 80 bar. Using the Peng-Robinson equation of state, with the critical temperature Tc = 304.13 K, the critical pressure Pc = 73.77 bar, the acentric factor ω = 0.224, the molar mass M = 44.01 g/mol and the gas constant R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), compute the following.

The compressibility factor Z, which is the ratio of the real molar volume to the ideal gas molar volume, dimensionless.

The molar volume of the gas, in cm³/mol.

The density of the gas, in kg/m³.

The molar volume that the ideal gas law gives at the same conditions, in cm³/mol.

At 80 bar, carbon dioxide is far from an ideal gas, so the ideal gas law gives a wrong volume. The Peng-Robinson equation corrects it, but to use it you must solve for the volume, which leads to a cubic equation. At the end, you will have a program that gives Z, the molar volume and the density, next to the ideal gas value.

## Model

There is no balance in this problem. The model is an equation of state, which relates the pressure, the molar volume and the temperature of the gas.

[eq-block] Peng-Robinson equation of state: 

 ⟦P = (R T)/(V − b) − (a)/(V^(2) + 2 b V − b^(2) )⟧ 

P — pressure of the gas, Pa

V — molar volume of the gas, the volume of one mole, m³/mol

T — temperature of the gas, K

a — attraction between the molecules, Pa·m⁶/mol²

b — volume occupied by the molecules themselves, m³/mol

The first term is the ideal gas law, RT/V, with the volume reduced by b, because the molecules take up space. The second term lowers the pressure, because the molecules attract each other. With a = 0 and b = 0, the equation becomes the ideal gas law.

[eq-block] Parameter a: 

 ⟦a = 0.45724 (R^(2) T_c^(2) )/(P_c ) α⟧ 

Tc, Pc — critical temperature and pressure, K and Pa

α — correction for the temperature, dimensionless; it equals 1 at Tc

Each substance has its own a, computed from its critical point. It depends on the temperature only through α.

[eq-block] Alpha function: 

 ⟦α = [ 1 + κ ( 1 − √((T)/(T_c )) ) ]^(2)⟧ 

κ — a factor that depends only on the substance, dimensionless

The square root of T/Tc measures how far the gas is from its critical temperature.

[eq-block] Kappa, from the acentric factor: 

 ⟦κ = 0.37464 + 1.54226 ω − 0.26992 ω^(2)⟧ 

ω — acentric factor, given in the problem, dimensionless

An empirical fit that turns the acentric factor into the number κ; it is the same for every temperature.

[eq-block] Parameter b: 

 ⟦b = 0.07780 (R T_c )/(P_c )⟧ 

Computed from the critical point only: it does not depend on the temperature.

[eq-block] Cubic form in Z: 

 ⟦Z^(3) − ( 1 − B ) Z^(2) + ( A − 3 B^(2) − 2 B ) Z − ( A B − B^(2) − B^(3) ) = 0⟧ 

 ⟦Z = (P V)/(R T) , A = (a P)/(( R T )^(2) ) , B = (b P)/(R T)⟧ 

Z — compressibility factor, PV/(RT), dimensionless; it is 1 for an ideal gas

A, B — dimensionless groups of the pressure, the temperature and the parameters

Writing V as ZRT/P in the equation of state and multiplying through removes the volume. What is left is a cubic in Z, whose coefficients depend only on A and B, which are known once P and T are.

### Compressibility factor as a function of pressure

[purpose] Write, once, the procedure that gives Z for any pressure: it builds the cubic, finds its roots and keeps the physical one.

```
def Z_of_P(P):
 """Compressibility factor at pressure P (Pa), at temperature T."""
 A = a * P / (R * T)**2 # dimensionless
 B = b * P / (R * T) # dimensionless
 c2 = -(1 - B) # coefficient of Z**2
 c1 = A - 3*B**2 - 2*B # coefficient of Z
 c0 = -(A*B - B**2 - B**3) # constant term
 roots = np.roots([1, c2, c1, c0]) # the three roots of the cubic
 real = roots[np.isreal(roots)].real # keep the real roots
 return real.max() # the largest real root
```
