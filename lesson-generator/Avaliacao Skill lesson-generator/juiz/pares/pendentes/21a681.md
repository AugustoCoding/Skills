# Jacketed batch reactor with an exothermic reaction

[problem] 

An exothermic liquid-phase reaction A → B, first order in A, is carried out in a jacketed batch reactor. The rate constant follows k = k0·exp(−E/(R·T)), with k0 = 6.0×10⁶ s⁻¹, E = 60 kJ/mol and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). The initial concentration of A is CA0 = 2000 mol/m³, the heat of reaction is ΔHr = −80 kJ/mol and the volumetric heat capacity of the mixture is ρcp = 4.0×10⁶ J/(m³·K). The jacket stays at Tj = 300 K and removes heat with UA/V = 2000 W/(m³·K). The reactor starts at T0 = 320 K.

The maximum temperature (K) and the time at which it occurs (min).

The conversion after 30 min (%).

The time to reach 95 % conversion (min).

The reaction releases heat, the heat speeds up the reaction, and the jacket pulls the temperature back toward 300 K; which effect wins changes with time, so the temperature goes up and then comes down. At the end you will have a Python program that integrates the mass and energy balances together, catches the temperature peak and the 95 % crossing exactly, and draws both curves.

## Model

### Mass balance of component A, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of A = in − out − consumption⟧ 

(principle) Moles of A are conserved except where the reaction destroys them. The control volume is the liquid inside the reactor.

 ⟦(d ( V ⋅ C_A ))/(d t) {accumulation} = 0 {in} − 0 {out} − r ⋅ V {consumption}⟧ 

CA — concentration of A in the liquid of the reactor, mol/m³

V — volume of liquid in the reactor, m³

r — rate of the reaction, moles of A consumed per m³ of liquid per second, mol/(m³·s)

t — time since the start of the batch, s

(translation) Each word becomes a molar flow of A, in mol/s. A batch reactor has no feed and no outlet, so "in" and "out" are zero.

 ⟦r = k ( T ) ⋅ C_A⟧ 

k — rate constant, which depends on the temperature of the liquid, s⁻¹

T — temperature of the liquid in the reactor, K

(kinetics) First order in A, as the problem states. The temperature enters through k, and that is what ties this balance to the energy balance.

 ⟦V = constant⟧ 

(assumption) The liquid barely changes density as A turns into B, so its volume stays put. If it did change, V would have to stay inside the derivative and be computed from the composition at each instant.

[final] 

 ⟦(d C_A )/(d t) {change of A} = − k ( T ) ⋅ C_A {consumption by reaction}⟧ 

(algebra) Kinetics substituted and everything divided by the constant V.

A disappears at a rate proportional to how much A is left. If T were fixed, this would be a plain exponential decay; because T changes, k changes during the batch, and the equation cannot be solved without knowing T(t) at the same time.

[eq-block] Arrhenius law: 

 ⟦k ( T ) = k_0 ⋅ exp ( − (E)/(R ⋅ T) )⟧ 

k0 — pre-exponential factor, 6.0×10⁶ s⁻¹

E — activation energy, 60 000 J/mol

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Given in the statement. With an activation energy this large, a few kelvin more make k noticeably larger: this strong sensitivity is what lets the heat of reaction feed on itself.

[eq-block] Conversion: 

 ⟦X = 1 − (C_A )/(C_A 0 )⟧ 

X — conversion of A, the fraction of the initial A that has already reacted, dimensionless (shown in %)

CA0 — concentration of A at the start of the batch, mol/m³

The definition the questions use: 95 % conversion means CA = 0.05·2000 = 100 mol/m³.

### Energy balance of the reactor contents, in watts

 ⟦accumulation of energy = heat released by reaction − heat removed by the jacket⟧ 

(principle) Conservation of energy for the same control volume, the liquid in the reactor. With no streams, no energy enters or leaves with matter; stirring work is neglected next to the heat of reaction.

 ⟦(d ( ρ c_p ⋅ V ⋅ T ))/(d t) {accumulation of energy} = ( − Δ H_r ) ⋅ r ⋅ V {heat of reaction} − U A ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {exchange with the jacket}⟧ 

ρcp — volumetric heat capacity of the mixture, the energy to heat 1 m³ of liquid by 1 K, J/(m³·K)

ΔHr — heat of reaction per mole of A reacted, negative because the reaction is exothermic, J/mol

UA — product of the overall heat transfer coefficient and the jacket area, W/K

Tj — temperature of the jacket fluid, K

(translation) Each word becomes a power, in W. The minus sign in (−ΔHr) turns the negative heat of reaction into a positive heat release.

 ⟦ρ c_p , V = constant⟧ 

(assumption) The heat capacity does not change with composition or temperature over the temperature range of this batch, and the volume is constant as in the mass balance. If ρcp varied, it would have to be evaluated at each instant and the energy balance written in enthalpy.

[final] 

 ⟦(d T)/(d t) {heating rate} = ( ( − Δ H_r ) ⋅ k ( T ) ⋅ C_A {generated, per m³} − (U A)/(V) ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {removed, per m³} )/(ρ c_p )⟧ 

(algebra) Constants taken out of the derivative, kinetics substituted, and everything divided by ρcp·V. The statement gives UA/V directly, so V itself is never needed.

The temperature rises while the heat generated per m³ exceeds the heat removed per m³ and falls when the jacket wins. The maximum temperature is exactly the instant when the two are equal, dT/dt = 0. Since the generated term contains CA, it shrinks as A runs out, so the jacket always wins in the end.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Numerical integration

The model is two differential equations that feed each other: the rate of change of CA depends on T, and that of T depends on CA, and both start from known values. To solve this kind of problem, we will use numerical integration: the computer advances the state in small time increments, using the equations to know where it is heading. It fits here because the exponential in k(T) rules out a closed-form solution, and the same tool can locate the peak and the 95 % crossing exactly. In Python, the function that does this is solve_ivp, from the library SciPy. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Balances as a Python function

[purpose] Turn the two model equations into a function that, given the time and the state, returns dCA/dt and dT/dt. This is what the integrator will call.

```
def balances(t, y, UAV):
 CA, T = y # unpack the state
 k = k0 * np.exp(-E / (R * T)) # rate constant, 1/s
 r = k * CA # reaction rate, mol/(m³·s)
 dCA = -r
 dT = (-dH * r - UAV * (T - Tj)) / rho_cp
 return [dCA, dT]
```

[how] def creates a function: a named recipe that receives values (its arguments, in parentheses) and gives back a result with return. The indented lines are its body. The arguments are fixed by solve_ivp: first the time t, then the state y, which always arrives as a list (here, two numbers: CA and T, in that order), then any extra values; UAV is passed as an extra so the check can rerun the same function with no jacket. CA, T = y is unpacking: it gives a name to each item of the list, in order. np.exp is the exponential. The return is a list with the two derivatives in the same order as the state. t is not used in the body, because nothing in the model depends explicitly on the clock, but it must be there because the integrator always sends it first.

[so-far] The program can evaluate the right-hand side of the model for any state.

[step] 

### Events: the instants the questions ask for

[purpose] Two of the answers are instants: when T stops rising and when X reaches 95 %. Instead of searching for them afterwards in a table of points, we let the integrator detect them during the integration.

```
def peak(t, y, UAV):
 return balances(t, y, UAV)[1] # dT/dt: zero at the peak
peak.direction = -1 # only when dT/dt goes + to −

def x95(t, y, UAV):
 return y[0] - 0.05 * CA0 # zero when X = 95 %
```

[how] An event is a function with the same arguments as balances that returns a number; solve_ivp watches its sign and, when it changes between two steps, finds the exact instant where it crosses zero. peak returns dT/dt: balances(...) gives the list of derivatives, and [1] takes its second item (Python counts from 0, so [0] is the first). peak.direction = -1 sets an attribute of the function, a label that solve_ivp reads: only crossings from positive to negative count, that is, a maximum and not a minimum. x95 returns CA − 100 mol/m³, which is zero exactly at 95 % conversion.

[so-far] The program knows what to watch for, but has not integrated anything yet.

[step] 

### Integration in time

[purpose] March the model from the initial state to 60 min, catching both events on the way.

```
sol = solve_ivp(balances, (0, t_end), [CA0, T0],
 args=(UAV,), events=[peak, x95],
 rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-6, dense_output=True)
```

[how] solve_ivp starts at t = 0 with the initial state, calls balances to get the derivatives, takes a time step, and repeats, a few hundred times in this case. At each call it passes the current time, the current state as a list, and then the contents of args. It picks the step size itself: short when the state changes fast (around the peak), long when it is calm. Its arguments:

[how] 

balances — the function itself, without parentheses: we hand over the recipe, and solve_ivp decides when to call it.

(0, t_end) — the time interval, from 0 to 3600 s.

[CA0, T0] — the initial state, in the same order the function unpacks: 2000 mol/m³ and 320 K.

args=(UAV,) — extra values added to every call, after t and y; here, the jacket coefficient. It must be a tuple (a fixed list written with parentheses), and the comma is what makes (UAV,) a tuple of one item instead of just a number in parentheses.

events=[peak, x95] — the functions to watch, from the previous step.

rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-6 — the tolerances: the error the integrator accepts at each step, relative to the size of each variable (one part in 10⁸) and absolute (10⁻⁶, in the units of the state). It shortens the step until its own error estimate is below them. They are this tight so that the printed decimals would not change with tighter values.

dense_output=True — keeps an interpolating formula between steps, so the solution can be read at any instant, such as exactly 30 min.

The result sol is an object that bundles everything: sol.t_events holds the instants of each event, sol.y_events the state at each of them, and sol.sol(t) evaluates the state at any time t.

### Why does the temperature rise at first, even with a jacket 20 K colder?

At the start, the heat removed per m³ is 2000·(320 − 300) = 40 000 W/m³, while the reaction, with all 2000 mol/m³ of A still present, releases more than that. The surplus heats the liquid, the warmer liquid reacts faster, and the Temperature curve steepens over the first minutes instead of flattening.

### What stops the temperature at 341.3 K?

Not the jacket getting stronger, but A running out: at the peak, at 13.7 min, the Conversion of A is already 86.9 %, so only 100 − 86.9 = 13.1 % of the reactant is left to release heat. From there, the heat generated per m³ falls below the heat removed and the Temperature slides back toward 300 K over the rest of the hour.

### Why does conversion go from 95 % to 99.2 % so slowly?

The Conversion of A passes 95 % at 18.0 min, during the hot stretch just after the peak; the last 99.2 − 95 = 4.2 percentage points take the next 30 − 18 = 12 min, because the reaction is now first order on a small CA and, at the same time, the cooling Temperature makes k smaller.

### Lower UA/V to 1000 W/(m³·K)

With half the cooling, the self-heating has more room: the Temperature peak climbs and the Conversion of A curve becomes almost a step. The peak moves very little in time, because the faster reaction also exhausts A sooner. # Exothermic batch reactor: maximum temperature and conversion

[problem] 

An exothermic liquid-phase reaction, A → B, is first order in reactant A. It runs in a jacketed batch reactor, with a constant liquid volume. The rate constant is k = 6.0×106 exp(−E/(RT)) s−1, with E = 60 kJ/mol and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). The initial concentration of A is CA0 = 2000 mol/m³. The heat of reaction is ΔH = −80 kJ/mol, and the volumetric heat capacity of the mixture is ρ·cp = 4.0×106 J/(m³·K). The jacket stays at Tj = 300 K and removes heat with UA/V = 2000 W/(m³·K). The reactor starts at T = 320 K.

What is the maximum temperature (K), and at what time does it occur (min)?

What is the conversion of A after 30 min (%)?

What time is needed to reach 95 % conversion (min)?

The reaction releases heat, which warms the liquid, while the jacket removes it. At the end, you will have a program that solves the two balances over 30 minutes, prints the three answers and draws the curves of reactant A and temperature against time.

## Model

### Mass balance of reactant A, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of A = in − out − consumption⟧ 

(principle) A is neither created nor destroyed, it only reacts. The control volume is the liquid inside the reactor.

 ⟦(d ( V ⋅ C_A ))/(d t) {accumulation} = − r_A ⋅ V {consumption}⟧ 

CA — concentration of reactant A in the reactor, mol/m³

V — volume of liquid in the reactor, m³

rA — rate at which A reacts, per volume of liquid, mol/(m³·s)

(translation) A batch has no flow, so the terms "in" and "out" are zero and drop out.

 ⟦r_A = k ⋅ C_A⟧ 

k — rate constant, in 1/s, which depends on temperature (see the Arrhenius law below)

(kinetics) First order in A, as the statement says: the rate is proportional to the concentration.

 ⟦V = constant⟧ 

(assumption) The liquid density does not change, so the volume stays the same. If the volume changed, a balance on the volume would be needed.

[final] 

 ⟦(d C_A )/(d t) {accumulation of A} = − k ⋅ C_A {first-order consumption}⟧ 

(algebra) Both sides are divided by V, which is constant.

The reaction consumes A at a rate proportional to what is left, so the concentration falls exponentially. Because k depends on temperature, the speed of this fall changes as the reactor heats up.

### Energy balance of the liquid, in joules

 ⟦accumulation of energy = heat generated by reaction − heat removed by the jacket⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. The control volume is the same liquid inside the reactor.

 ⟦ρ ⋅ c_p ⋅ V ⋅ (d T)/(d t) {accumulation of energy} = ( − ΔH ) ⋅ r_A ⋅ V {heat generated} − UA ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {heat removed}⟧ 

T — temperature of the liquid in the reactor, K

ρ, cp — density and specific heat of the mixture, so that ρ·cp = 4.0×106 J/(m³·K)

ΔH — heat of reaction per mole of A, negative for an exothermic reaction, J/mol

UA — overall heat transfer coefficient times jacket area, W/K

Tj — temperature of the jacket, K

(translation) Each word becomes a term: the stored energy, the heat released by the reaction, and the heat that leaves through the jacket.

 ⟦T = the same at every point of the liquid⟧ 

(assumption) The liquid is perfectly stirred, so one temperature describes it. If it were not, the temperature would change from point to point and each point would need its own balance.

 ⟦ρ, c p and T j are constant⟧ 

(assumption) The mixture keeps its density and heat capacity, and the jacket stays at 300 K, as the statement says. If the jacket temperature changed, Tj would be a function of time in the last term.

[final] 

 ⟦ρ ⋅ c_p ⋅ (d T)/(d t) {accumulation} = ( − ΔH ) ⋅ k ⋅ C_A {heat generated per volume} − (UA)/(V) ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {heat removed per volume}⟧ 

(algebra) Both sides are divided by V, and rA is replaced by kCA.

The reaction heats the liquid at the rate (−ΔH)·k·CA, which is positive because ΔH is negative. The jacket removes heat in proportion to how far the liquid is above 300 K. While the heat generated is larger, the temperature rises; when the two terms are equal, it peaks; after that, the jacket wins and the temperature falls.

[eq-block] Arrhenius law: 

 ⟦k = k_0 ⋅ exp ( − (E)/(R ⋅ T) )⟧ 

k0 — pre-exponential factor, 6.0×106 1/s

E — activation energy, J/mol

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

The rate constant grows quickly with temperature: the reaction is slow at 320 K and fast at higher temperatures. This is the coupling between the two balances: the temperature sets the speed of the reaction, and the reaction sets the temperature.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Numerical integration

This is an initial value problem: two equations that say how the concentration and the temperature change with time, starting from known values. To solve this kind of problem, we will use numerical integration, which advances the solution in time by small steps, each one adding the change computed from the balances. The temperature enters the exponential of the rate constant, so no formula gives the state at 30 min: the steps are the only path. In Python, the function that does this is solve_ivp from the library SciPy. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Rate constant and the two balances

[purpose] The program needs two pieces of the model: the rate constant at any temperature, and the two derivatives, which tell how fast the concentration and the temperature change at any instant.

```
def k(T):
 # rate constant at temperature T (K), in 1/s
 return k0 * np.exp(-E / (R * T))

def balances(t, y):
 # derivatives of y = [C_A, T] at time t (s)
 CA, T = y # unpack the state
 r = k(T) * CA # rate, mol/(m³·s)
 dCA = -r # mass, mol/(m³·s)
 dT = ((-dH) * r - UA_V * (T - Tj)) / rhocp # energy, K/s
 return [dCA, dT]
```

[how] def k(T): defines a function: a named piece of code that receives a value, here the temperature T, and gives back a result with return. Inside it, np.exp is the exponential function, and k0 * np.exp(...) is the Arrhenius law of the model.

[how] def balances(t, y): is the function that the integrator will call. It receives two arguments: t, the instant in seconds, and y, the state, which here is a list of two numbers, [C_A, T]. The line CA, T = y is called "unpacking": it takes the first item of the list and gives it the name CA, and the second one, named T. The function returns a list with the two derivatives, in the same order.

[so-far] The program can now compute, for any temperature and concentration, how fast each quantity changes. Nothing has been solved yet.

### Solving for 30 minutes and marking 95 % conversion

[purpose] The integrator starts from the initial values and advances to 30 min. It also watches for the moment the conversion reaches 95 %, and records the solution at many instants.

```
def reached_95(t, y):
 # zero when the conversion of A reaches 95 %
 return y[0] - 0.05 * CA0
reached_95.terminal = False # keep integrating

t_end = 30 * 60.0 # 30 min, in s
t_grid = np.linspace(0, t_end, 18001) # one point per 0.1 s
sol = solve_ivp(balances, [0, t_end], [CA0, T0],
 method="LSODA", rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-8,
 t_eval=t_grid, events=reached_95)
```

[how] def reached_95(t, y): is an "event function". The integrator calls it at each step; while y[0], the concentration, is above 5 % of CA0, it is positive. When it changes sign, the integrator knows that 95 % conversion happened between two steps and finds that instant. The line reached_95.terminal = False adds an attribute to the function: False tells the integrator not to stop there.

[how] The call to solve_ivp has these arguments, in order:

[how] 

balances: the function with the derivatives, which the integrator calls many times, each time with the current t and the state y.

[0, t_end]: the time span, from 0 to 1800 s.

[CA0, T0]: the initial state, the concentration and the temperature, in the order balances expects.

method="LSODA": the integration method. It adapts the step size on its own and copes with the sharp changes of an exothermic reaction.

rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-8: tolerances, the largest relative and absolute error the integrator accepts in each step. Small values give more precise numbers, at the cost of more steps.

t_eval=t_grid: the instants at which the solution is recorded, one every 0.1 s. Without it, the integrator would record only the steps it chose.

events=reached_95: the event function from above.

[so-far] The program has integrated the balances for 30 minutes and stored the solution in sol.

### Checking the conversion by a second route

[purpose] Two routes must agree. The concentration of A is CA = CA0 exp(−∫k dt), so the integral of the rate constant along the solved temperature must give the same conversion.

```
# Check: integrate the rate constant along the solved T(t)
kt = k(T) # k at each instant
I = np.sum(np.diff(sol.t) * (kt[1:] + kt[:-1]) / 2) # trapezoid
print("Conversion from the rate integral: %.1f %%"
 % (100 * (1 - np.exp(-I))))
```

[how] kt = k(T) applies the function k to the whole array T at once: NumPy does this for every element, which is why no loop is needed. np.diff(sol.t) gives the interval between consecutive instants; kt[1:] and kt[:-1] are the values from the second item to the end and from the start to the next-to-last, so their sum over two is the average over each interval (the trapezoid rule). np.sum adds the contributions of all intervals, giving the integral I. np.exp(-I) gives the fraction of A that is left.

[so-far] The program now prints the conversion computed a second way. If it matches the one printed before, the solution is trustworthy.
