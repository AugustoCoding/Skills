# Compressibility of carbon dioxide from the Peng-Robinson equation

[problem] 

Carbon dioxide is stored at T = 320 K and P = 80 bar. Using the Peng-Robinson equation of state, with the critical temperature Tc = 304.13 K, the critical pressure Pc = 73.77 bar, the acentric factor ω = 0.224, the molar mass M = 44.01 g/mol and the gas constant R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), compute the following.

The compressibility factor Z, which is the ratio of the real molar volume to the ideal gas molar volume, dimensionless.

The molar volume of the gas, in cm³/mol.

The density of the gas, in kg/m³.

The molar volume that the ideal gas law gives at the same conditions, in cm³/mol.

At 80 bar, carbon dioxide is far from an ideal gas, so the ideal gas law gives a wrong volume. The Peng-Robinson equation corrects it, but to use it you must solve for the volume, which leads to a cubic equation. At the end, you will have a program that gives Z, the molar volume and the density, next to the ideal gas value.

## Model

There is no balance in this problem. The model is an equation of state, which relates the pressure, the molar volume and the temperature of the gas.

[eq-block] Peng-Robinson equation of state: 

 ⟦P = (R T)/(V − b) − (a)/(V^(2) + 2 b V − b^(2) )⟧ 

P — pressure of the gas, Pa

V — molar volume of the gas, the volume of one mole, m³/mol

T — temperature of the gas, K

a — attraction between the molecules, Pa·m⁶/mol²

b — volume occupied by the molecules themselves, m³/mol

The first term is the ideal gas law, RT/V, with the volume reduced by b, because the molecules take up space. The second term lowers the pressure, because the molecules attract each other. With a = 0 and b = 0, the equation becomes the ideal gas law.

[eq-block] Parameter a: 

 ⟦a = 0.45724 (R^(2) T_c^(2) )/(P_c ) α⟧ 

Tc, Pc — critical temperature and pressure, K and Pa

α — correction for the temperature, dimensionless; it equals 1 at Tc

Each substance has its own a, computed from its critical point. It depends on the temperature only through α.

[eq-block] Alpha function: 

 ⟦α = [ 1 + κ ( 1 − √((T)/(T_c )) ) ]^(2)⟧ 

κ — a factor that depends only on the substance, dimensionless

The square root of T/Tc measures how far the gas is from its critical temperature.

[eq-block] Kappa, from the acentric factor: 

 ⟦κ = 0.37464 + 1.54226 ω − 0.26992 ω^(2)⟧ 

ω — acentric factor, given in the problem, dimensionless

An empirical fit that turns the acentric factor into the number κ; it is the same for every temperature.

[eq-block] Parameter b: 

 ⟦b = 0.07780 (R T_c )/(P_c )⟧ 

Computed from the critical point only: it does not depend on the temperature.

[eq-block] Cubic form in Z: 

 ⟦Z^(3) − ( 1 − B ) Z^(2) + ( A − 3 B^(2) − 2 B ) Z − ( A B − B^(2) − B^(3) ) = 0⟧ 

 ⟦Z = (P V)/(R T) , A = (a P)/(( R T )^(2) ) , B = (b P)/(R T)⟧ 

Z — compressibility factor, PV/(RT), dimensionless; it is 1 for an ideal gas

A, B — dimensionless groups of the pressure, the temperature and the parameters

Writing V as ZRT/P in the equation of state and multiplying through removes the volume. What is left is a cubic in Z, whose coefficients depend only on A and B, which are known once P and T are.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Polynomial roots

The problem is to find the value of Z that makes a cubic polynomial equal to zero, that is, a polynomial root. To solve this kind of problem, we will use polynomial root finding: the roots are computed directly from the coefficients, with no initial guess and no trial and error. It fits because a cubic has up to three roots, and only the real one has a physical meaning, so the program has to select it. In Python, the function that does this is roots, from the library NumPy. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Compressibility factor as a function of pressure

[purpose] Write, once, the procedure that gives Z for any pressure: it builds the cubic, finds its roots and keeps the physical one.

```
def Z_of_P(P):
 """Compressibility factor at pressure P (Pa), at temperature T."""
 A = a * P / (R * T)**2 # dimensionless
 B = b * P / (R * T) # dimensionless
 c2 = -(1 - B) # coefficient of Z**2
 c1 = A - 3*B**2 - 2*B # coefficient of Z
 c0 = -(A*B - B**2 - B**3) # constant term
 roots = np.roots([1, c2, c1, c0]) # the three roots of the cubic
 real = roots[np.isreal(roots)].real # keep the real roots
 return real.max() # the largest real root
```

# Peng–Robinson equation of state for CO₂

[problem] 

Carbon dioxide is stored at temperature T = 320 K and pressure P = 80 bar. Its critical temperature is Tc = 304.13 K, its critical pressure is Pc = 73.77 bar, its acentric factor is ω = 0.224, its molar mass is M = 44.01 g/mol, and the gas constant is R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). The gas follows the Peng–Robinson equation of state. The exercise asks for:

the compressibility factor Z of the gas;

the molar volume v, in cm³/mol;

the density ρ, in kg/m³;

the molar volume that the ideal gas law would give at the same conditions, in cm³/mol.

At 80 bar and 320 K, CO₂ is a dense supercritical fluid, and the ideal gas law is far from the truth. You will write a short Python program that solves the Peng–Robinson equation, compares it with the ideal gas, and plots how the compressibility factor changes with pressure.

## Model

[eq-block] Peng–Robinson equation of state: 

 ⟦P = (R ⋅ T)/(v − b) − (a)/(v^(2) + 2 b v − b^(2) )⟧ 

v — molar volume of the gas, m³/mol

b — volume per mole that the molecules themselves occupy, m³/mol

a — strength of the attraction between molecules, Pa·m⁶/mol²

The first term is a repulsion: the molecules cannot be squeezed below the volume b, so the pressure it gives is higher than the ideal gas pressure. The second term is the attraction between molecules, which lowers the pressure. The ideal gas has neither effect.

[eq-block] Parameters a and b: 

 ⟦a = 0.45724 ⋅ (R^(2) T_c^(2) )/(P_c ) ⋅ α b = 0.07780 ⋅ (R T_c )/(P_c ) α = [ 1 + κ ( 1 − √(T / T_c ) ) ]^(2) κ = 0.37464 + 1.54226 ω − 0.26992 ω^(2)⟧ 

α — correction of the attraction with temperature, dimensionless; it equals 1 at the critical temperature

κ — constant of the fluid, computed from its acentric factor, dimensionless

These are the correlations that define the Peng–Robinson model; the numerical coefficients belong to the model itself. They let us estimate a and b for any fluid from its critical point and its acentric factor, a measure of how non-spherical the molecule is.

[eq-block] Cubic equation for Z: 

 ⟦Z^(3) − ( 1 − B ) Z^(2) + ( A − 3 B^(2) − 2 B ) Z − ( A B − B^(2) − B^(3) ) = 0⟧ 

Z — compressibility factor of the gas, Pv/(RT), dimensionless; it is 1 for an ideal gas

A — dimensionless attraction parameter, aP/(RT)²

B — dimensionless volume parameter, bP/(RT)

The equation of state is written with P given and v unknown, but v appears in a way that is awkward to isolate. The usual trick is to substitute v = ZRT/P and multiply everything out. What remains is a polynomial of degree three in Z, with coefficients that depend only on A and B. A cubic has three roots. At this temperature and pressure only one is real, and it is the physical one. In other conditions there may be three real roots: the smallest is the liquid, the largest is the vapor, and the one in the middle has no physical meaning. For a gas, we take the largest.

[eq-block] Molar volume and density: 

 ⟦v = (Z R T)/(P) ρ = (M)/(v) v_ideal = (R T)/(P)⟧ 

ρ — density of the gas, kg/m³

videal — molar volume given by the ideal gas law, m³/mol

Once Z is known, the definition of Z gives the molar volume. The density is the mass of one mole divided by the volume of one mole. The ideal gas volume is the same formula with Z = 1.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Roots of a polynomial

Finding Z means finding the values that make a cubic polynomial equal to zero. To solve this kind of problem, we will use the roots of a polynomial: a direct calculation that returns all the roots at once, with no guess and no iteration to tune. It fits here because the cubic has a closed-form solution, and we need to see all three roots to pick the gas one. In Python, the function that does this is np.roots, from the library NumPy. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Function that solves the cubic

[purpose] Pack the whole calculation of Z into a function of temperature and pressure, because we will need it again for many pressures to draw the figure.

```
def pr_z(T, P):
 """Compressibility factor of CO2 by Peng-Robinson."""
 kappa = 0.37464 + 1.54226*w - 0.26992*w**2
 alpha = (1 + kappa*(1 - np.sqrt(T/Tc)))**2
 a = 0.45724 * R**2 * Tc**2 / Pc * alpha # Pa m6/mol2
 b = 0.07780 * R * Tc / Pc # m3/mol
 A = a*P / (R*T)**2 # dimensionless
 B = b*P / (R*T) # dimensionless
 coef = [1, -(1 - B), A - 3*B**2 - 2*B, -(A*B - B**2 - B**3)]
 roots = np.roots(coef)
 real = roots[np.abs(roots.imag) < 1e-9].real
 return real.max(), a, b, A, B
```

[how] 

def pr_z(T, P): defines a function. It receives two values, which inside it are called T and P (they are independent from the T and P of step 1). The indented lines below are its body. The text in triple quotes is a description, not executed.

** raises to a power; np.sqrt is the square root. The lines that follow are the correlations of the model, written as in the equations.

coef is a list, values between square brackets separated by commas. It holds the four coefficients of the cubic, from Z³ down to the constant term.

np.roots(coef) receives the list of coefficients, highest power first, and returns an array with the three roots. Some may be complex numbers, which have a real part and an imaginary part (.imag).

roots[np.abs(roots.imag) < 1e-9] keeps only the roots whose imaginary part is essentially zero, that is, below 10⁻⁹ in absolute value (np.abs). The square brackets act as a filter. 1e-9 is a tolerance for rounding noise in the computer. .real then drops the zero imaginary part.

real.max() picks the largest of the real roots, the gas root.

return hands back five values at once, separated by commas (a tuple): Z, a, b, A and B.

def pr_z(T, P):
 """Compressibility factor of CO2 by Peng-Robinson."""
 kappa = 0.37464 + 1.54226*w - 0.26992*w**2
 alpha = (1 + kappa*(1 - np.sqrt(T/Tc)))**2
 a = 0.45724 * R**2 * Tc**2 / Pc * alpha # Pa m6/mol2
 b = 0.07780 * R * Tc / Pc # m3/mol
 A = a*P / (R*T)**2 # dimensionless
 B = b*P / (R*T) # dimensionless
 coef = [1, -(1 - B), A - 3*B**2 - 2*B, -(A*B - B**2 - B**3)]
 roots = np.roots(coef)
 real = roots[np.abs(roots.imag) < 1e-9].real
 return real.max(), a, b, A, B

Z, a, b, A, B = pr_z(T, P)
v = Z * R * T / P # molar volume, m3/mol
rho = M / v # density, kg/m3

print(f"a = {a:.4f} Pa m6/mol2, b = {b*1e6:.2f} cm3/mol")

### Why is Z so far below 1 at 80 bar?

At 320 K and 80 bar the compressibility factor is 0.5559, so the real gas occupies 184.9 cm³/mol where the ideal gas would need 332.6 cm³/mol, a difference of 332.6 − 184.9 = 147.7 cm³/mol. The temperature is only a little above the critical 304.13 K and the pressure is close to the critical 73.77 bar, so the molecules are close enough for the attraction term to pull the gas into a smaller volume than the ideal gas law predicts.

### Does Z keep falling as the pressure rises?

No. The compressibility factor falls from almost 1 to a minimum of 0.344 at 122.5 bar and then rises again to 0.422 at 200 bar. Beyond the minimum the molecules are so packed that the repulsion, the volume b, starts to win over the attraction.

### Where does the ideal gas law work?

At the left of the figure, where the pressure is low. At 0.1 bar, Z is 0.9996, so Z, ideal gas and Z, Peng–Robinson are the same line for practical purposes. The ideal gas law is a good model for a dilute gas, and a poor one for a stored, compressed gas.

### Take T up to 400 K

The curve of Z, Peng–Robinson moves up and the dip becomes shallow: the molecules move faster and the attraction matters less. The readout shows Z = 0.8443 at 80 bar, and the curve no longer has a minimum inside the range, it just decreases up to 200 bar.
