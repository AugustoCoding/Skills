# Adiabatic flame temperature of methane

[problem] 

Methane (CH4) is burned completely with dry air, 21 % O2 and 79 % N2 (molar), in an adiabatic combustor. Fuel and air enter at T0 = 298.15 K. The reaction is CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O(g), and its heat of combustion at 298.15 K, with water as vapor, is −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4. The ideal-gas heat capacities follow Cp/R = A + B·T + D/T², with T in K and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); the coefficients are in the parameter table below. The excess of air is ε, relative to the amount needed for complete combustion.

Calculate the adiabatic flame temperature (K) with 20 % excess air.

What would it be with 100 % excess air (K)?

What percentage of excess air gives a flame temperature of 1500 K?

All the heat of the reaction stays in the gases leaving the combustor, and the more air is fed, the more nitrogen and oxygen share that heat. At the end you will have a program that finds the flame temperature for any excess of air, the excess that gives 1500 K, and a curve of temperature against excess.

## Model

### Mass balance of each species, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of i = in − out + generation by reaction⟧ 

(principle) Matter of each species i is not created or destroyed, except by the reaction. The control volume is the combustor, and the basis of calculation is 1 mol of CH4 fed.

 ⟦0 = n_i 0 {in} − n_i {out} + ν_i ⋅ ξ {generation}⟧ 

ni0 — moles of species i fed to the combustor, per mol of CH4, mol

ni — moles of species i leaving the combustor, per mol of CH4, mol

νi — stoichiometric coefficient of i in the reaction: negative for reactants, positive for products, dimensionless

ξ — extent of reaction, the number of times the reaction takes place per mol of CH4 fed, mol

(translation) (assumption) Steady state, so the accumulation is zero. If the combustor were starting up, the accumulation would not vanish and we would need differential equations in time.

 ⟦ξ = 1 mol , n_O_2 0 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) , n_N_2 0 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) r⟧ 

ε — excess of air, as a fraction of the air needed for complete combustion, dimensionless (0.2 means 20 %)

r — moles of N2 per mole of O2 in the air, 79/21, dimensionless

(assumption) (definition) Complete combustion: the 1 mol of CH4 fed is entirely consumed, so ξ = 1 mol. If the combustion were partial, ξ would be smaller and CH4 and CO would appear in the products. The stoichiometric O2 is 2 mol, and the excess multiplies it by (1 + ε).

[final] 

 ⟦n_C O_2 = 1 , n_H_2 O = 2 n_O_2 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) − 2 {fed minus consumed} = 2 ε n_N_2 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) r {passes through}⟧ 

(algebra) Apply the balance of the first step to each species, with the data of the previous steps.

These are the gases leaving the combustor for each mole of methane burned. The excess O2 and the inert N2 do not take part in the reaction, but they still absorb heat: the more air, the more moles to heat, which is what lowers the temperature.

### Energy balance of the combustor, in joules

 ⟦enthalpy flow in = enthalpy flow out⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. For a flowing gas at steady state, with no work and with negligible kinetic and potential energy, what enters as enthalpy leaves as enthalpy. The control volume is the combustor, per mole of CH4 fed.

 ⟦0 = Δ H_c {combustion at T₀} + ∑ n_i ⋅ ΔH_i ( T ) {heating the products}⟧ 

ΔHc — heat of combustion at 298.15 K, with water as vapor, per mol of CH4, −802.6 kJ/mol

T — temperature of the gases leaving the combustor, the flame temperature, K

ΔHi — enthalpy change of 1 mol of gas i when heated from 298.15 K to T, J/mol

(translation) Enthalpy does not depend on the path, so we choose one: first the reaction takes place at 298.15 K, where the heat of combustion is known, and then the products are heated from 298.15 K to T. Fuel and air enter at 298.15 K, so the reactants need no heating term.

 ⟦ΔH_i ( T ) = R [ A_i ( T − T_0 ) + (B_i )/(2) ( T^(2) − T_0^(2) ) − D_i ( (1)/(T) − (1)/(T_0 ) ) ]⟧ 

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Ai, Bi, Di — coefficients of the heat capacity of gas i; A is dimensionless, B in 1/K and D in K²

(definition) The enthalpy change is the integral of the heat capacity, ΔHi = ∫ Cp,i dT from T₀ to T, with Cp,i/R = Ai + BiT + Di/T². Integrating term by term gives the expression above.

 ⟦Q_loss = 0⟧ 

Qloss — heat lost through the walls of the combustor, per mol of CH4, J/mol

(assumption) Adiabatic combustor, which is why the balance above has no heat term. With heat loss, a term −Qloss would appear and the flame temperature would be lower. We also treat the gases as ideal, so the enthalpy depends only on temperature and not on pressure.

[final] 

 ⟦∑ n_i ( ε ) ⋅ ΔH_i ( T ) {heat absorbed by the products} = Q = − Δ H_c {heat released by the reaction}⟧ 

Q — heat released by the combustion of 1 mol of CH4, 802 600 J/mol

(algebra) Move the heat of combustion to the other side, with Q = −ΔHc = 802.6 kJ/mol = 802 600 J/mol, and write the moles ni as functions of ε, from the mass balance.

All the heat released by the reaction ends up as sensible heat of the products: CO2, H2O, the excess O2 and the N2 of the air. The unknown is T, which appears in a polynomial and in a 1/T term, so it cannot be isolated by hand. The excess ε, on the other hand, appears linearly, and that gives a direct formula for the third question, below.

[eq-block] Excess air for a given temperature: 

 ⟦ε = (Q − ΔH_CO2 ( T ) − 2 ΔH_H2O ( T ) − 2 r ΔH_N2 ( T ))/(2 ΔH_O2 ( T ) + 2 r ΔH_N2 ( T ))⟧ 

Fixing the temperature at the target value in the final equation and putting the moles in terms of ε, the terms without ε (CO2, H2O and the N2 that accompanies the stoichiometric O2) go to the numerator, and the terms multiplied by ε (O2 and N2 in excess) to the denominator. All the enthalpy changes are evaluated at the target temperature.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Root finding

The flame temperature is the value of T that makes the energy balance true, an equation we cannot solve by hand. This kind of problem is called finding the root of a function, the point where it crosses zero. To solve it, we will use bracketing root finding (Brent's method): we give an interval where the function changes sign, and the method narrows it step by step until the zero is located with the precision wanted. It fits because the residual changes sign between 298.15 K and very high temperatures. In Python, the function that does this is brentq, from the library scipy.optimize. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Heating one mole of gas

[purpose] Turn the integral of the heat capacity into a function that gives the heat needed to take 1 mol of a gas from T₀ to T.

```
def dH(gas, T):
 """Enthalpy change of 1 mol of gas from T0 to T, J/mol."""
 A, B, D = coef[gas]
 return R * (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))
```

[how] 
def dH(gas, T): defines a function named dH. It receives the name of a gas (a text such as "CO2") and a temperature, and hands back a number with return.
The text between triple quotes is a docstring, a description of the function.
A, B, D = coef[gas] is unpacking: the tuple of three numbers found in the dictionary is split into three variables.
**2 is the square. The expression is the closed form of the integral of Cp from T0 to T, multiplied by R to get J/mol.
The line break inside the parentheses is allowed and only keeps the line short.

### Energy balance as a residual

[purpose] Write the balance as a function that is zero when the temperature is the right one: the heat absorbed by the products minus the heat released.

```
def moles(eps):
 """Moles leaving the combustor per mol of CH4 burned."""
 return {"CO2": 1.0, "H2O": 2.0, "O2": 2 * eps,
 "N2": 2 * (1 + eps) * r}

 """Heat absorbed by the products minus heat released, J."""
```

The function returns the heat absorbed minus Q. It is negative if T is too low (the products could still absorb more) and positive if T is too high.

### Complete program

```
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
from scipy.integrate import quad
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

R = 8.314 # gas constant, J/(mol*K)
T0 = 298.15 # feed and reference temperature, K
Q = 802600.0 # heat released per mol of CH4, J/mol
r = 79 / 21 # mol of N2 per mol of O2 in the air

# coefficients (A, B, D) of Cp/R for each gas
coef = {
 "CO2": (5.457, 1.045e-3, -1.157e5),
 "H2O": (3.470, 1.450e-3, 0.121e5),
 "N2": (3.280, 0.593e-3, 0.040e5),
 "O2": (3.639, 0.506e-3, -0.227e5),
}

def dH(gas, T):
 """Enthalpy change of 1 mol of gas from T0 to T, J/mol."""
 A, B, D = coef[gas]
 return R * (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))

def moles(eps):
 """Moles leaving the combustor per mol of CH4 burned."""
 return {"CO2": 1.0, "H2O": 2.0, "O2": 2 * eps,
 "N2": 2 * (1 + eps) * r}

def residual(T, eps):
 """Heat absorbed by the products minus heat released, J."""
 n = moles(eps)
 heat = sum(n[g] * dH(g, T) for g in n)
 return heat - Q

for pct in (0, 20, 100, 200):
 T = brentq(residual, T0, 5000, args=(pct / 100,))
 print(f"Excess {pct:3d} %: T = {T:.1f} K")

T_target = 1500.0 # desired flame temperature, K
a = (dH("CO2", T_target) + 2 * dH("H2O", T_target)
 + 2 * r * dH("N2", T_target))
b = 2 * dH("O2", T_target) + 2 * r * dH("N2", T_target)
eps = (Q - a) / b
print(f"Excess air for {T_target:.0f} K: {100 * eps:.1f} %")

def cp(T, A, B, D):
 """Heat capacity of one gas at temperature T, J/(mol*K)."""
 return R * (A + B * T + D / T**2)

n = moles(eps)
heat = 0.0
for g in n:
 A, B, D = coef[g]
 heat += n[g] * quad(cp, T0, T_target, args=(A, B, D))[0]
print(f"Check, heat absorbed at {T_target:.0f} K: "
 f"{heat / 1000:.1f} kJ")

pct = np.linspace(0, 200, 81)
Tad = [brentq(residual, T0, 5000, args=(p / 100,)) for p in pct]
plt.plot(pct, Tad, label="Flame temperature")
plt.axhline(T_target, linestyle="--", label="Target temperature")
plt.xlabel("Excess air (%)")
plt.ylabel("Temperature (K)")
plt.legend()
plt.show()
```

### Why does the temperature fall as more air is fed?

The heat released is the same, 802.6 kJ per mol of CH4, but it is shared among more moles of gas. The flame temperature is 2311.2 K with no excess, 2066.4 K with 20 %, 1486.4 K with 100 % and 1140.3 K with 200 %.

### Where does the flame temperature reach the target?

The flame temperature crosses the target temperature of 1500 K at 97.2 % excess. At 100 % it is already 13.6 K below the target (1500 − 1486.4), which shows that the crossing is just before that point.

### Is the fall in temperature the same along the whole curve?

No. The first 20 % of excess lowers the flame temperature by 244.8 K (2311.2 − 2066.4), while the 100 points from 100 % to 200 % lower it by 346.1 K (1486.4 − 1140.3). The curve therefore flattens: the loss per percent of added air is much larger at the beginning than at the end.
