# Exothermic batch reactor: maximum temperature and conversion

[problem] 

An exothermic liquid-phase reaction, A → B, is first order in reactant A. It runs in a jacketed batch reactor, with a constant liquid volume. The rate constant is k = 6.0×106 exp(−E/(RT)) s−1, with E = 60 kJ/mol and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). The initial concentration of A is CA0 = 2000 mol/m³. The heat of reaction is ΔH = −80 kJ/mol, and the volumetric heat capacity of the mixture is ρ·cp = 4.0×106 J/(m³·K). The jacket stays at Tj = 300 K and removes heat with UA/V = 2000 W/(m³·K). The reactor starts at T = 320 K.

What is the maximum temperature (K), and at what time does it occur (min)?

What is the conversion of A after 30 min (%)?

What time is needed to reach 95 % conversion (min)?

The reaction releases heat, which warms the liquid, while the jacket removes it. At the end, you will have a program that solves the two balances over 30 minutes, prints the three answers and draws the curves of reactant A and temperature against time.

## Model

### Mass balance of reactant A, in moles

 ⟦accumulation of A = in − out − consumption⟧ 

(principle) A is neither created nor destroyed, it only reacts. The control volume is the liquid inside the reactor.

 ⟦(d ( V ⋅ C_A ))/(d t) {accumulation} = − r_A ⋅ V {consumption}⟧ 

CA — concentration of reactant A in the reactor, mol/m³

V — volume of liquid in the reactor, m³

rA — rate at which A reacts, per volume of liquid, mol/(m³·s)

(translation) A batch has no flow, so the terms "in" and "out" are zero and drop out.

 ⟦r_A = k ⋅ C_A⟧ 

k — rate constant, in 1/s, which depends on temperature (see the Arrhenius law below)

(kinetics) First order in A, as the statement says: the rate is proportional to the concentration.

 ⟦V = constant⟧ 

(assumption) The liquid density does not change, so the volume stays the same. If the volume changed, a balance on the volume would be needed.

[final] 

 ⟦(d C_A )/(d t) {accumulation of A} = − k ⋅ C_A {first-order consumption}⟧ 

(algebra) Both sides are divided by V, which is constant.

The reaction consumes A at a rate proportional to what is left, so the concentration falls exponentially. Because k depends on temperature, the speed of this fall changes as the reactor heats up.

### Energy balance of the liquid, in joules

 ⟦accumulation of energy = heat generated by reaction − heat removed by the jacket⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. The control volume is the same liquid inside the reactor.

 ⟦ρ ⋅ c_p ⋅ V ⋅ (d T)/(d t) {accumulation of energy} = ( − ΔH ) ⋅ r_A ⋅ V {heat generated} − UA ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {heat removed}⟧ 

T — temperature of the liquid in the reactor, K

ρ, cp — density and specific heat of the mixture, so that ρ·cp = 4.0×106 J/(m³·K)

ΔH — heat of reaction per mole of A, negative for an exothermic reaction, J/mol

UA — overall heat transfer coefficient times jacket area, W/K

Tj — temperature of the jacket, K

(translation) Each word becomes a term: the stored energy, the heat released by the reaction, and the heat that leaves through the jacket.

 ⟦T = the same at every point of the liquid⟧ 

(assumption) The liquid is perfectly stirred, so one temperature describes it. If it were not, the temperature would change from point to point and each point would need its own balance.

 ⟦ρ, c p and T j are constant⟧ 

(assumption) The mixture keeps its density and heat capacity, and the jacket stays at 300 K, as the statement says. If the jacket temperature changed, Tj would be a function of time in the last term.

[final] 

 ⟦ρ ⋅ c_p ⋅ (d T)/(d t) {accumulation} = ( − ΔH ) ⋅ k ⋅ C_A {heat generated per volume} − (UA)/(V) ⋅ ( T − T_j ) {heat removed per volume}⟧ 

(algebra) Both sides are divided by V, and rA is replaced by kCA.

The reaction heats the liquid at the rate (−ΔH)·k·CA, which is positive because ΔH is negative. The jacket removes heat in proportion to how far the liquid is above 300 K. While the heat generated is larger, the temperature rises; when the two terms are equal, it peaks; after that, the jacket wins and the temperature falls.

[eq-block] Arrhenius law: 

 ⟦k = k_0 ⋅ exp ( − (E)/(R ⋅ T) )⟧ 

k0 — pre-exponential factor, 6.0×106 1/s

E — activation energy, J/mol

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

The rate constant grows quickly with temperature: the reaction is slow at 320 K and fast at higher temperatures. This is the coupling between the two balances: the temperature sets the speed of the reaction, and the reaction sets the temperature.

k0 | 6.0×106 | 1/s | given |

[technique] 

[technique-name] Numerical integration

This is an initial value problem: two equations that say how the concentration and the temperature change with time, starting from known values. To solve this kind of problem, we will use numerical integration, which advances the solution in time by small steps, each one adding the change computed from the balances. The temperature enters the exponential of the rate constant, so no formula gives the state at 30 min: the steps are the only path. In Python, the function that does this is solve_ivp from the library SciPy. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Rate constant and the two balances

[purpose] The program needs two pieces of the model: the rate constant at any temperature, and the two derivatives, which tell how fast the concentration and the temperature change at any instant.

```
def k(T):
 # rate constant at temperature T (K), in 1/s
 return k0 * np.exp(-E / (R * T))

def balances(t, y):
 # derivatives of y = [C_A, T] at time t (s)
 CA, T = y # unpack the state
 r = k(T) * CA # rate, mol/(m³·s)
 dCA = -r # mass, mol/(m³·s)
 dT = ((-dH) * r - UA_V * (T - Tj)) / rhocp # energy, K/s
 return [dCA, dT]
```

[how] def k(T): defines a function: a named piece of code that receives a value, here the temperature T, and gives back a result with return. Inside it, np.exp is the exponential function, and k0 * np.exp(...) is the Arrhenius law of the model.

[how] def balances(t, y): is the function that the integrator will call. It receives two arguments: t, the instant in seconds, and y, the state, which here is a list of two numbers, [C_A, T]. The line CA, T = y is called "unpacking": it takes the first item of the list and gives it the name CA, and the second one, named T. The function returns a list with the two derivatives, in the same order.

[so-far] The program can now compute, for any temperature and concentration, how fast each quantity changes. Nothing has been solved yet.

[step] 

### Solving for 30 minutes and marking 95 % conversion

[purpose] The integrator starts from the initial values and advances to 30 min. It also watches for the moment the conversion reaches 95 %, and records the solution at many instants.

```
def reached_95(t, y):
 # zero when the conversion of A reaches 95 %
 return y[0] - 0.05 * CA0
reached_95.terminal = False # keep integrating

t_end = 30 * 60.0 # 30 min, in s
t_grid = np.linspace(0, t_end, 18001) # one point per 0.1 s
sol = solve_ivp(balances, [0, t_end], [CA0, T0],
 method="LSODA", rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-8,
 t_eval=t_grid, events=reached_95)
```

[how] def reached_95(t, y): is an "event function". The integrator calls it at each step; while y[0], the concentration, is above 5 % of CA0, it is positive. When it changes sign, the integrator knows that 95 % conversion happened between two steps and finds that instant. The line reached_95.terminal = False adds an attribute to the function: False tells the integrator not to stop there.

[how] The call to solve_ivp has these arguments, in order:

[how] 

balances: the function with the derivatives, which the integrator calls many times, each time with the current t and the state y.

[0, t_end]: the time span, from 0 to 1800 s.

[CA0, T0]: the initial state, the concentration and the temperature, in the order balances expects.

method="LSODA": the integration method. It adapts the step size on its own and copes with the sharp changes of an exothermic reaction.

rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-8: tolerances, the largest relative and absolute error the integrator accepts in each step. Small values give more precise numbers, at the cost of more steps.

t_eval=t_grid: the instants at which the solution is recorded, one every 0.1 s. Without it, the integrator would record only the steps it chose.

events=reached_95: the event function from above.

[so-far] The program has integrated the balances for 30 minutes and stored the solution in sol.

### Checking the conversion by a second route

[purpose] Two routes must agree. The concentration of A is CA = CA0 exp(−∫k dt), so the integral of the rate constant along the solved temperature must give the same conversion.

```
# Check: integrate the rate constant along the solved T(t)
kt = k(T) # k at each instant
I = np.sum(np.diff(sol.t) * (kt[1:] + kt[:-1]) / 2) # trapezoid
print("Conversion from the rate integral: %.1f %%"
 % (100 * (1 - np.exp(-I))))
```

[how] kt = k(T) applies the function k to the whole array T at once: NumPy does this for every element, which is why no loop is needed. np.diff(sol.t) gives the interval between consecutive instants; kt[1:] and kt[:-1] are the values from the second item to the end and from the start to the next-to-last, so their sum over two is the average over each interval (the trapezoid rule). np.sum adds the contributions of all intervals, giving the integral I. np.exp(-I) gives the fraction of A that is left.

[so-far] The program now prints the conversion computed a second way. If it matches the one printed before, the solution is trustworthy.

### Complete program

```
import numpy as np # arrays and math functions
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt # figures
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp # the ODE integrator

# Data of the problem, in SI units
R = 8.314 # gas constant, J/(mol·K)
E = 60000.0 # activation energy, J/mol
k0 = 6.0e6 # pre-exponential factor, 1/s
CA0 = 2000.0 # initial concentration of A, mol/m³
dH = -80000.0 # heat of reaction, J/mol (negative: exothermic)
rhocp = 4.0e6 # ρ·cp of the mixture, J/(m³·K)
UA_V = 2000.0 # UA/V of the jacket, W/(m³·K)
Tj = 300.0 # jacket temperature, K
T0 = 320.0 # initial temperature of the reactor, K

def k(T):
 # rate constant at temperature T (K), in 1/s
 return k0 * np.exp(-E / (R * T))

def balances(t, y):
 # derivatives of y = [C_A, T] at time t (s)
 CA, T = y # unpack the state
 r = k(T) * CA # rate, mol/(m³·s)
 dCA = -r # mass, mol/(m³·s)
 dT = ((-dH) * r - UA_V * (T - Tj)) / rhocp # energy, K/s
 return [dCA, dT]

def reached_95(t, y):
 # zero when the conversion of A reaches 95 %
 return y[0] - 0.05 * CA0
reached_95.terminal = False # keep integrating

t_end = 30 * 60.0 # 30 min, in s
t_grid = np.linspace(0, t_end, 18001) # one point per 0.1 s
sol = solve_ivp(balances, [0, t_end], [CA0, T0],
 method="LSODA", rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-8,
 t_eval=t_grid, events=reached_95)

t = sol.t / 60 # time in min
CA, T = sol.y # the two rows
i = np.argmax(T) # index of the peak
print("Maximum temperature: %.1f K" % T[i])
print("Time of the maximum: %.1f min" % t[i])
conv_max = 100 * (1 - CA[i] / CA0)
print("Conversion at the maximum: %.1f %%" % conv_max)
print("Temperature after 30 min: %.1f K" % T[-1])
print("Conversion after 30 min: %.1f %%"
 % (100 * (1 - CA[-1] / CA0)))
if len(sol.t_events[0]) > 0: # did the event happen?
 t95 = sol.t_events[0][0] / 60
 print("Time to 95%% conversion: %.2f min" % t95)
else:
 print("95% conversion is not reached in 30 min")

# Check: integrate the rate constant along the solved T(t)
kt = k(T) # k at each instant
I = np.sum(np.diff(sol.t) * (kt[1:] + kt[:-1]) / 2) # trapezoid
print("Conversion from the rate integral: %.1f %%"
 % (100 * (1 - np.exp(-I))))

fig, ax1 = plt.subplots()
ax1.plot(t, CA, color="C0", label="Reactant A")
ax1.set_xlabel("Time (min)")
ax1.set_ylabel("Concentration of A (mol/m³)")
ax2 = ax1.twinx()
ax2.plot(t, T, color="C3", label="Temperature")
ax2.set_ylabel("Temperature (K)")
plt.show()
```
