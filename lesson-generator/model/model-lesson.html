Reactors · initial value ODE system

Start-up of a CSTR

A continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR) with V = 100 L of liquid starts full of pure solvent. From t = 0 on, it receives F = 5 L/min of a feed with CA0 = 2.0 mol/L of reactant A, which turns into product B by a first-order reaction with k = 0.1 min⁻¹. The outlet flow equals the inlet flow. Calculate:

  • the steady-state concentration of A in the reactor;
  • the time the reactor takes to reach 95% of that concentration.

Right after start-up there is no reactant inside at all; little by little A comes in, part of it reacts, and the concentrations head toward a steady state. You will leave with a program that integrates the balances of A and B, finds that instant by itself and checks its own result against the exact solution.

Model

Mass balance of component A, in moles

  1. accumulation of A=in−out−consumption

    principleA does not appear or disappear for no reason. This is a component mass balance, written in moles because the reaction counts molecules. Taking the liquid inside the reactor as the control volume, what accumulates there is what comes in, minus what goes out, minus what the reaction consumes. Each term is measured in moles of A per minute.

  2. d(V⋅CA)dt accumulation = F⋅CA0in − F⋅CAout − rA⋅Vconsumption
    • V — volume of liquid inside the reactor, L
    • CA — concentration of reactant A inside the reactor, which is also that of the outlet stream, because stirring keeps the tank uniform, mol/L
    • F — volumetric flow of the feed, equal to that of the outlet, L/min
    • CA0 — concentration of A in the feed stream, mol/L
    • rA — rate at which the reaction consumes A: how many moles of A react per liter of liquid every minute, mol/(L·min)

    translationEach word becomes a molar flow. The amount of A in the reactor is V·CA. The inlet stream brings F·CA0, and the outlet takes F·CA, because the liquid leaves with the composition of the tank. The reaction consumes rA in each liter, and there are V liters.

  3. rA=k⋅CA
    • k — rate constant of the reaction A → B: the fraction of the A present that reacts every minute, 1/min

    kineticsThe reaction is first order, as the problem states: the rate is proportional to the concentration of A. With twice as much A in the tank, twice as much reacts per minute.

  4. V⋅dCAdt =F⋅(CA0−CA) −k⋅CA⋅V

    assumptionThe volume of liquid does not change, because the same flow F goes in and out. So V comes out of the derivative as a constant. If the tank were filling up, this step would not hold, and the volume would need a balance of its own.

  5. dCAdt accumulation of A = 1τ(CA0−CA) in minus out − k⋅CA consumption by the reaction
    • τ — residence time: how long, on average, a portion of liquid stays in the reactor before leaving, equal to V/F, min

    algebradefinitionDividing everything by V and calling V/F τ, we reach the model equation for A.

    The concentration of A inside the tank changes for two reasons. The stream brings A at CA0 and takes it away at CA, and this exchange, divided by the residence time, pushes the tank toward the feed. At the same time, the reaction consumes A at a rate proportional to what is already there. The steady state is the point where the two effects cancel out.

Mass balance of component B, in moles

  1. accumulation of B=in−out+generation

    principleAnother component mass balance, in moles, with the same control volume. For B, the reaction appears with a plus sign, because it produces B.

  2. d(V⋅CB)dt accumulation = 0in − F⋅CBout + rA⋅Vgeneration
    • CB — concentration of product B inside the reactor and at the outlet, mol/L

    translationB does not come in with the feed, so the inlet is zero. Since the reaction A → B turns one mole into one mole, the generation of B is exactly the consumption of A, rA·V.

  3. dCBdt accumulation of B = −CBτ leaves with the stream + k⋅CA formation by the reaction

    kineticsassumptionalgebraThe same three steps as in the balance of A, at once: rA = k·CA, constant volume, and everything divided by V.

    B is only born from the reaction and only leaves with the outlet stream. The formation term is the same k·CA that appears with the opposite sign in the equation of A: it is what ties the two balances together.

Numerical integration

The model says how much each concentration changes at every instant, and we know where they start: from zero, at start-up. To solve this kind of problem, we will use numerical integration: starting from the initial value, the computer advances time in very short steps and, at each one, uses the equations to update the concentrations. It fits here because it only needs the equations and the starting point. In Python, the function that does this is solve_ivp, from the SciPy library. See the next section to learn how to use it.

Code

Parameters

First, store the data of the problem in variables, so the rest of the program uses them by name instead of repeating loose numbers.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

V = 100.0      # volume of liquid in the reactor, L
F = 5.0        # feed flow (equal to the outlet), L/min
CA0 = 2.0      # concentration of A in the feed, mol/L
k = 0.1        # rate constant of A -> B, 1/min
tau = V / F    # residence time, min
t_end = 60.0   # simulation horizon, min

import brings in the libraries the program will use: NumPy, for calculations with lists of numbers; the function solve_ivp, from SciPy, which does the numerical integration; and Matplotlib, for the plot. The as np gives the library a short nickname, and from … import brings only the function we need. Each following line creates a variable: the name to the left of the = now holds the value on the right. The # starts a comment, which Python ignores; that is where the unit goes. The residence time is calculated, not typed: if you change V or F, it follows by itself.

The program knows the reactor: all the data are stored with names, and nothing has been calculated yet.

Balances of A and B

solve_ivp needs to ask, for any instant and any concentrations, how fast each concentration is changing. That is exactly what the two model equations answer; so we write them in a function.

def derivatives(t, C):
    CA, CB = C
    r = k * CA                   # reaction rate, mol/(L·min)
    dCA = (CA0 - CA) / tau - r   # in - out - consumption
    dCB = (0.0 - CB) / tau + r   # no B in - B out + formation
    return [dCA, dCB]

def creates a function: derivatives is its name, and t and C are what it receives, the instant and the list with the two concentrations. The body is the indented block below the colon. CA, CB = C unpacks the list into two variables with the names of the equation. The next three lines are the model, term by term; the 0.0 makes it visible that no B comes in, instead of looking forgotten. return gives back the two derivatives, in the same order as the concentrations. The order of what the function receives, first t and then C, is the one solve_ivp requires, even though t does not appear in the calculations. The rate r is calculated once and used in both lines: all A consumed shows up as B.

The model became code: given the concentrations at an instant, the program can tell where they are heading.

Target: 95% of the steady state

The problem asks for the instant at which A reaches 95% of the steady state. For that, we need the steady-state value and a way for solve_ivp to signal when that value is reached.

CA_ss = CA0 / (1 + k * tau)      # dCA/dt = 0 solved for CA
CB_ss = k * tau * CA_ss          # dCB/dt = 0 solved for CB

def reaches_95(t, C):
    return C[0] - 0.95 * CA_ss

The steady state comes from one line of algebra: set the derivative of A to zero and isolate CA; the same for B. The function reaches_95 receives the same t and C as the balance function and returns the distance between CA (the C[0], the first item of the list) and the target: negative before, positive after. Handed to solve_ivp as an event, it is checked at every advance in time, and the instant at which it changes sign is located precisely: it is the instant asked for.

The program knows where each concentration will settle and has a way to recognize the instant asked for. What remains is to advance in time.

Integration

Now, the numerical integration itself: take the concentrations from zero to the end of the horizon, recording the instant of the event on the way.

sol = solve_ivp(derivatives, (0, t_end), [0.0, 0.0],
                rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-10,
                events=reaches_95, dense_output=True)
t95 = sol.t_events[0][0]
CA_end, CB_end = sol.y[:, -1]

solve_ivp receives three arguments in order and the others by name:

  • derivatives: the balance function, handed over without parentheses, because solve_ivp is the one that calls it.
  • (0, t_end): the time interval, from the start to the horizon.
  • [0.0, 0.0]: the initial concentrations of A and B.
  • rtol and atol: the tolerances, the error it accepts at each advance, relative to the size of the concentration and absolute, in mol/L. The smaller they are, the shorter the advances and the more exact the result; with these values, the instant of the event comes out right far beyond the two decimals the lesson quotes, and the next step measures that.
  • events=reaches_95: switches on the event of the previous step.
  • dense_output=True: keeps a formula that stitches the calculated points together, to evaluate the solution at any instant without integrating again.

The answer goes into sol: sol.t_events[0][0] is the first instant at which the first event fired, and sol.y[:, -1] is the last column of sol.y, the two concentrations at the end, unpacked into two variables.

The problem is solved: t95 holds the instant asked for. Before trusting it, let us check it.

Checking against the exact solution

A number that comes from a numerical method needs an independent proof. Here there is one: the equation of A has an exact solution, and the two can be compared.

t = np.linspace(0, t_end, 601)
CA_exact = CA_ss * (1 - np.exp(-(1 / tau + k) * t))
deviation = np.max(np.abs(sol.sol(t)[0] - CA_exact))

np.linspace(0, t_end, 601) creates 601 equally spaced instants between 0 and t_end. The next line calculates the exact solution of A at all of them at once: with NumPy, a calculation done with a list applies to each of its elements. sol.sol(t) evaluates the numerical solution at the same instants (it is the formula kept by dense_output), and the [0] takes the row of A. np.abs removes the sign of the differences, and np.max keeps the largest.

The exact solution is an exponential that rises to the steady-state CA with rate 1/τ + k. solve_ivp does not know that. If the two agree at 601 points, to the ninth decimal, the code of the balances and the numerical integration are right, and not just "plausible".

The program solves the problem and checks its own result. What remains is to show it.

Printing and drawing

Finally, show the numbers the problem asks for and the start-up curve.

print(f"Steady-state CA: {CA_ss:.3f} mol/L")
print(f"A reaches 95% of the steady state at t = {t95:.2f} min")
print(f"At {t_end:.0f} min: CA = {CA_end:.3f} mol/L, CB = {CB_end:.3f} mol/L "
      f"({CB_end / CB_ss:.0%} of the steady state of B, {CB_ss:.3f} mol/L)")
print(f"Largest deviation from the exact solution: {deviation:.1e} mol/L")

CA, CB = sol.sol(t)
plt.plot(t, CA, label="Reactant A")
plt.plot(t, CB, "--", label="Product B")
plt.xlabel("Time (min)")
plt.ylabel("Concentration (mol/L)")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

A text with f before the quotes inserts the value of each variable written between braces; after the colon comes the format: .3f is three decimals, .2f two, .0% a percentage with no decimals, .1e scientific notation. Two texts side by side inside the parentheses become one, which lets the long line break. In the plot, sol.sol(t) gives the two concentrations at the 601 instants; plt.plot draws each curve, with its name in label and "--" for the dashed one; xlabel, ylabel and legend add the names; and plt.show() shows the figure. The 601 points make the curve smooth: the instants at which solve_ivp stopped are few and uneven, and with them the curve would come out broken right at the start.

The program is complete: it prints the answers and draws the start-up of the reactor.

Complete program

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- parameters ---
V = 100.0      # volume of liquid in the reactor, L
F = 5.0        # feed flow (equal to the outlet), L/min
CA0 = 2.0      # concentration of A in the feed, mol/L
k = 0.1        # rate constant of A -> B, 1/min
tau = V / F    # residence time, min
t_end = 60.0   # simulation horizon, min

# --- balances of A and B ---
def derivatives(t, C):
    CA, CB = C
    r = k * CA                   # reaction rate, mol/(L·min)
    dCA = (CA0 - CA) / tau - r   # in - out - consumption
    dCB = (0.0 - CB) / tau + r   # no B in - B out + formation
    return [dCA, dCB]

# --- the target: 95% of the steady state of A ---
CA_ss = CA0 / (1 + k * tau)      # dCA/dt = 0 solved for CA
CB_ss = k * tau * CA_ss          # dCB/dt = 0 solved for CB

def reaches_95(t, C):
    return C[0] - 0.95 * CA_ss

# --- integration ---
sol = solve_ivp(derivatives, (0, t_end), [0.0, 0.0],
                rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-10,
                events=reaches_95, dense_output=True)
t95 = sol.t_events[0][0]
CA_end, CB_end = sol.y[:, -1]

# --- check against the exact solution of A ---
t = np.linspace(0, t_end, 601)
CA_exact = CA_ss * (1 - np.exp(-(1 / tau + k) * t))
deviation = np.max(np.abs(sol.sol(t)[0] - CA_exact))

# --- results ---
print(f"Steady-state CA: {CA_ss:.3f} mol/L")
print(f"A reaches 95% of the steady state at t = {t95:.2f} min")
print(f"At {t_end:.0f} min: CA = {CA_end:.3f} mol/L, CB = {CB_end:.3f} mol/L "
      f"({CB_end / CB_ss:.0%} of the steady state of B, {CB_ss:.3f} mol/L)")
print(f"Largest deviation from the exact solution: {deviation:.1e} mol/L")

CA, CB = sol.sol(t)
plt.plot(t, CA, label="Reactant A")
plt.plot(t, CB, "--", label="Product B")
plt.xlabel("Time (min)")
plt.ylabel("Concentration (mol/L)")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

The six steps together, in order, ready to run. It is the same program you build in the editor by bringing in step after step.

Result

At steady state, CA = 0.667 mol/L: two thirds of the A that comes in is converted.

The reactor reaches 95% of that value at t = 19.97 min.

The program also prints CB = 1.234 mol/L at 60 min (93% of the 1.333 mol/L steady state of B) and a largest deviation of 1.7·10⁻⁹ mol/L from the exact solution.

  1. Why does product B take longer than reactant A?

    Reactant A rises and settles around 20 min, while product B keeps rising: at 60 min it is still at 93% of its steady state. The difference lies in the balances. A adjusts through the exchange with the stream and through the reaction at the same time, with rate 1/τ + k. B is only removed by the stream, with rate 1/τ, which in this reactor is three times smaller.

  2. What does CA0 change in the start-up time?

    Nothing. Doubling the feed concentration doubles the steady state, but the whole curve scales with it, and the 95% still falls at the same instant, because that instant only depends on 1/τ + k.

  3. Take k up to 0.30 1/min

    Reactant A settles much lower and sooner: the faster reaction consumes more A before it leaves, and the same reaction speeds up the balance between inflow and consumption.

    Change k = 0.1 to k = 0.3 and run: the program prints 0.286 mol/L and 95% at 8.56 min.

  4. Take F down to 2 L/min

    The residence time goes to 50 min, and reactant A settles lower, because it stays longer inside to react. In exchange, the start-up takes longer. It is the trade-off of every CSTR: more conversion costs a slower response.

    Change F = 5.0 to F = 2.0: the program prints 0.333 mol/L and 95% at 24.96 min.
