# Adiabatic flame temperature of methane

[problem] 

Methane is burned completely, CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, with 20% excess dry air (21% O2 and 79% N2, molar) in an adiabatic combustor. Fuel and air enter at T0 = 298.15 K. The heat of combustion at 298.15 K, with water as vapor, is ΔH°c = −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4. The ideal-gas heat capacities follow Cp/R = A + B·T + D/T² (T in K, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)):

CO2: A = 5.457, B = 1.045×10⁻³, D = −1.157×10⁵

H2O: A = 3.470, B = 1.450×10⁻³, D = 0.121×10⁵

N2: A = 3.280, B = 0.593×10⁻³, D = 0.040×10⁵

O2: A = 3.639, B = 0.506×10⁻³, D = −0.227×10⁵

Questions:

Calculate the adiabatic flame temperature with 20% excess air (K).

What would it be with 100% excess air (K)?

What percentage of excess air gives a flame temperature of 1500 K?

All the heat the reaction releases stays in the gases that leave the combustor: the products and the air that did not react. The more excess air, the more gas shares the same heat, and the cooler the flame. At the end you will have a program that finds the flame temperature for any excess air, and the excess air for any flame temperature.

## Model

### Mass balance of each species, in moles per mol of CH4

 ⟦out = in + generation by the reaction⟧ 

(principle) Each species is conserved except for what the reaction makes or consumes. The control volume is the combustor, at steady state, so nothing accumulates; the basis is 1 mol of CH4 fed.

 ⟦n_i {leaves} = n_i , 0 {enters} + ν_i ⋅ ξ {made by the reaction}⟧ 

ni — moles of species i that leave the combustor per mol of CH4 fed, mol

ni,0 — moles of species i that enter per mol of CH4 fed, mol

νi — stoichiometric coefficient of i in CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O: −1 for CH4, −2 for O2, +1 for CO2, +2 for H2O, 0 for N2

ξ — extent of reaction, the moles of CH4 that react per mol of CH4 fed, mol

(translation) Each word becomes a number of moles of species i.

 ⟦ξ = 1⟧ 

(assumption) Combustion is complete: all the CH4 becomes CO2 and H2O. If it were not, unburned CH4 or CO would leave, less heat would be released, and the flame would be cooler.

 ⟦n_O_2 , 0 = 2 ( 1 + e ) n_N_2 , 0 = (79)/(21) ⋅ 2 ( 1 + e )⟧ 

e — fraction of excess air, the O2 fed beyond the 2 mol the reaction needs, divided by those 2 mol: 0.20 for 20% excess, dimensionless

(definition) Excess air is measured against the stoichiometric O2, 2 mol per mol of CH4. Each mol of O2 brings 79/21 = 3.762 mol of N2, the molar ratio in air.

[final] 

 ⟦n_CO_2 = 1 {from the carbon} n_H_2 O = 2 {from the hydrogen} n_O_2 = 2 e {oxygen not used} n_N_2 = (79)/(21) ⋅ 2 ( 1 + e ) {inert, as it entered}⟧ 

(algebra) The balance of each species with ξ = 1 and the feed above; CH4 leaves with zero moles.

This is the composition of the gas that leaves the combustor. Only the O2 and N2 depend on the excess air, and both grow with it: excess air adds gas that must be heated without adding any heat.

### Overall energy balance of the combustor, in J per mol of CH4

 ⟦energy out with the stream = energy in with the stream + heat − shaft work⟧ 

(principle) First law for an open system at steady state. The control volume is the combustor, with fuel and air entering and the hot gases leaving.

 ⟦H_out ( T ) {enthalpy of the products} = H_in ( T_0 ) {enthalpy of fuel and air} + Q {heat} − W_s {shaft work}⟧ 

T — adiabatic flame temperature, the temperature of the gas leaving the combustor, K

Hout, Hin — enthalpy of the stream leaving and of the streams entering, per mol of CH4, J

T0 — temperature at which fuel and air enter, 298.15 K

Q, Ws — heat received and shaft work done, per mol of CH4, J

(translation) The energy carried by a stream is its enthalpy; changes of kinetic and potential energy are negligible next to a heat of combustion.

 ⟦Q = 0 W_s = 0 ⇒ H_out ( T ) − H_in ( T_0 ) = 0⟧ 

(assumption) The combustor is adiabatic and has no moving shaft. If it lost heat to the walls, the gas would leave cooler than the adiabatic flame temperature, which is therefore the highest the gas can reach.

 ⟦Δ H_c^(°) {react at T₀} + ∑ {i} n_i ∫_T_0^(T) C_p , i d T {heat the products from T₀ to T} = 0⟧ 

ΔH°c — heat of combustion at T0, water as vapor, −802.6 kJ per mol of CH4

Cp,i — ideal-gas heat capacity of product i, J/(mol·K)

(algebra) Enthalpy is a state function, so the change from inlet to outlet can follow any path. The convenient one: react at T0, where the heat of combustion is known, then heat the products from T0 to T.

 ⟦∫_T_0^(T) (C_p , i )/(R) d T = A_i ( T − T_0 ) + (B_i )/(2) ( T^(2) − T_0^(2) ) − D_i ( (1)/(T) − (1)/(T_0 ) )⟧ 

(algebra) The heat capacity correlation (below) integrated term by term: the integral of D/T² is −D/T.

[final] 

 ⟦Δ H_c^(°) {heat released} + R ∑ {i} n_i [ A_i ( T − T_0 ) + (B_i )/(2) ( T^(2) − T_0^(2) ) − D_i ( (1)/(T) − (1)/(T_0 ) ) ] {heat absorbed by the outlet gas} = 0⟧ 

(algebra) The integral of the previous step, multiplied by R, placed in the balance, with the ni of the mass balance.

The heat the reaction releases is exactly the heat that warms the outlet gas from T0 to T. The unknown T appears as T, T² and 1/T at once, so there is no formula that isolates it. The excess air e, on the other hand, appears only in the ni, and in a linear way.

[eq-block] Ideal-gas heat capacity: 

 ⟦(C_p , i )/(R) = A_i + B_i ⋅ T + (D_i )/(T^(2) )⟧ 

Ai, Bi, Di — correlation constants of gas i, dimensionless, 1/K and K², given in the statement

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Given by the statement. Heat capacities grow with temperature, which is why a single average value would not be exact between T0 and the flame temperature.

[eq-block] Excess air for a chosen flame temperature: 

 ⟦e = (− Δ H_c^(°) − S_0 )/(S_1 − S_0 )⟧ 

S0 — heat absorbed by the outlet gas, the second term of the energy balance, computed with e = 0 at the chosen temperature, J per mol of CH4

S1 — the same, with e = 1, J per mol of CH4

With T fixed, the heat absorbed is a straight line in e, S0 + (S1 − S0)·e, because the moles of O2 and N2 grow linearly with e. Setting it equal to −ΔH°c gives this formula, which answers the third question directly.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Root finding

The energy balance is a single equation in a single unknown, T, that cannot be rearranged into a formula. To solve this kind of problem, we will use root finding: we write the balance as a function, the residual, that is zero at the answer, and a method shrinks an interval where it changes sign. It fits because the residual is negative at T0 and positive at 4000 K, so one root lies in between; the third question needs only the formula for e. In Python, the function that does this is brentq, from the library SciPy. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Composition of the outlet gas

[purpose] Turn the mass balance into a function: given the excess air, it returns the moles of each gas that leave per mol of CH4.

 """Moles of each gas leaving per mol of CH4, for excess e."""

### Energy balance as a residual

[purpose] Write the energy balance as a function of T that is zero at the flame temperature: the residual that the root finder will drive to zero.

```
def heat_up(T, n):
 """Enthalpy to heat the products n from T0 to T, J/mol CH4."""
 total = 0.0
 for gas, moles in n.items():
 A, B, D = cp[gas]
 integral = (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))
 total += moles * R * integral
 return total

def residual(T, e):
 """Energy balance: zero at the adiabatic flame temperature."""
 return dHc + heat_up(T, products(e))
```

[how] heat_up receives a temperature T, in K, and a composition n (the dictionary from products), and returns the second term of the energy balance. for gas, moles in n.items(): is a loop: n.items() gives the pairs (name, moles) one at a time, and the indented lines run once per gas, with gas and moles taking the values of that pair. A, B, D = cp[gas] is unpacking: the three numbers of the tuple go into three names at once. T**2 is T squared. total += … adds to total the heat absorbed by this gas; the expression is split over two lines, which Python accepts inside parentheses. residual is the energy balance: heat of combustion (negative) plus heat absorbed (positive). It receives T first, because the root finder will vary this argument, and the excess air e second.

[so-far] For a guessed temperature, the program says how far the energy balance is from closing: negative if the guess is too cold, positive if too hot.

### How hot can a methane flame get with this air?

The flame temperature is highest at the left end of the curve, with stoichiometric air (0% excess): 2311.2 K, as the program prints. No excess O2 leaves, and the 802.6 kJ heat only the CO2, the H2O and the N2 that the air cannot avoid bringing.

### How much does going from 20% to 100% excess air cost?

The flame temperature falls from 2066.4 K to 1486.4 K, that is, 2066.4 − 1486.4 = 580.0 K. The heat released is the same 802.6 kJ in both cases; what changes is the amount of gas sharing it, since every extra mol of O2 brings 79/21 mol of N2 with it.

### Why does the curve flatten at high excess air?

Up to 100% the flame temperature loses 580.0 K; from 100% to 200% it goes from 1486.4 K to 1140.3 K. Each new mol of air is a smaller fraction of a gas stream that is already large, so it dilutes the heat less. Also, the colder the gas, the smaller its heat capacity, which slows the fall further.

The flame temperature meets the 1500 K target at 97.2% excess air, just to the left of 100%, which is consistent with 100% giving 1486.4 K, slightly below 1500 K. The readout below the figure solves the same formula as the program.

 ⟦n_C O_2 = 1 , n_H_2 O = 2 n_O_2 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) − 2 {fed minus consumed} = 2 ε n_N_2 = 2 ( 1 + ε ) r {passes through}⟧ 

(algebra) Apply the balance of the first step to each species, with the data of the previous steps.

These are the gases leaving the combustor for each mole of methane burned. The excess O2 and the inert N2 do not take part in the reaction, but they still absorb heat: the more air, the more moles to heat, which is what lowers the temperature.

### Energy balance of the combustor, in joules

 ⟦enthalpy flow in = enthalpy flow out⟧ 

(principle) Energy is conserved. For a flowing gas at steady state, with no work and with negligible kinetic and potential energy, what enters as enthalpy leaves as enthalpy. The control volume is the combustor, per mole of CH4 fed.

 ⟦0 = Δ H_c {combustion at T₀} + ∑ n_i ⋅ ΔH_i ( T ) {heating the products}⟧ 

ΔHc — heat of combustion at 298.15 K, with water as vapor, per mol of CH4, −802.6 kJ/mol

T — temperature of the gases leaving the combustor, the flame temperature, K

ΔHi — enthalpy change of 1 mol of gas i when heated from 298.15 K to T, J/mol

(translation) Enthalpy does not depend on the path, so we choose one: first the reaction takes place at 298.15 K, where the heat of combustion is known, and then the products are heated from 298.15 K to T. Fuel and air enter at 298.15 K, so the reactants need no heating term.

 ⟦ΔH_i ( T ) = R [ A_i ( T − T_0 ) + (B_i )/(2) ( T^(2) − T_0^(2) ) − D_i ( (1)/(T) − (1)/(T_0 ) ) ]⟧ 

R — gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)

Ai, Bi, Di — coefficients of the heat capacity of gas i; A is dimensionless, B in 1/K and D in K²

(definition) The enthalpy change is the integral of the heat capacity, ΔHi = ∫ Cp,i dT from T₀ to T, with Cp,i/R = Ai + BiT + Di/T². Integrating term by term gives the expression above.

 ⟦Q_loss = 0⟧ 

Qloss — heat lost through the walls of the combustor, per mol of CH4, J/mol

(assumption) Adiabatic combustor, which is why the balance above has no heat term. With heat loss, a term −Qloss would appear and the flame temperature would be lower. We also treat the gases as ideal, so the enthalpy depends only on temperature and not on pressure.

[final] 

 ⟦∑ n_i ( ε ) ⋅ ΔH_i ( T ) {heat absorbed by the products} = Q = − Δ H_c {heat released by the reaction}⟧ 

Q — heat released by the combustion of 1 mol of CH4, 802 600 J/mol

(algebra) Move the heat of combustion to the other side, with Q = −ΔHc = 802.6 kJ/mol = 802 600 J/mol, and write the moles ni as functions of ε, from the mass balance.

All the heat released by the reaction ends up as sensible heat of the products: CO2, H2O, the excess O2 and the N2 of the air. The unknown is T, which appears in a polynomial and in a 1/T term, so it cannot be isolated by hand. The excess ε, on the other hand, appears linearly, and that gives a direct formula for the third question, below.

[eq-block] Excess air for a given temperature: 

 ⟦ε = (Q − ΔH_CO2 ( T ) − 2 ΔH_H2O ( T ) − 2 r ΔH_N2 ( T ))/(2 ΔH_O2 ( T ) + 2 r ΔH_N2 ( T ))⟧ 

Fixing the temperature at the target value in the final equation and putting the moles in terms of ε, the terms without ε (CO2, H2O and the N2 that accompanies the stoichiometric O2) go to the numerator, and the terms multiplied by ε (O2 and N2 in excess) to the denominator. All the enthalpy changes are evaluated at the target temperature.

[technique] 

[technique-name] Root finding

The flame temperature is the value of T that makes the energy balance true, an equation we cannot solve by hand. This kind of problem is called finding the root of a function, the point where it crosses zero. To solve it, we will use bracketing root finding (Brent's method): we give an interval where the function changes sign, and the method narrows it step by step until the zero is located with the precision wanted. It fits because the residual changes sign between 298.15 K and very high temperatures. In Python, the function that does this is brentq, from the library scipy.optimize. See the next section to learn how to use it.

### Heating one mole of gas

[purpose] Turn the integral of the heat capacity into a function that gives the heat needed to take 1 mol of a gas from T₀ to T.

```
def dH(gas, T):
 """Enthalpy change of 1 mol of gas from T0 to T, J/mol."""
 A, B, D = coef[gas]
 return R * (A * (T - T0) + B / 2 * (T**2 - T0**2)
 - D * (1 / T - 1 / T0))
```

[how] 
def dH(gas, T): defines a function named dH. It receives the name of a gas (a text such as "CO2") and a temperature, and hands back a number with return.
The text between triple quotes is a docstring, a description of the function.
A, B, D = coef[gas] is unpacking: the tuple of three numbers found in the dictionary is split into three variables.
**2 is the square. The expression is the closed form of the integral of Cp from T0 to T, multiplied by R to get J/mol.
The line break inside the parentheses is allowed and only keeps the line short.

[so-far] The program can now compute, for any gas and any temperature, how much heat 1 mol of it absorbs when heated from 298.15 K.

[step] 

### Energy balance as a residual

[purpose] Write the balance as a function that is zero when the temperature is the right one: the heat absorbed by the products minus the heat released.

```
def moles(eps):
 """Moles leaving the combustor per mol of CH4 burned."""
 return {"CO2": 1.0, "H2O": 2.0, "O2": 2 * eps,
 "N2": 2 * (1 + eps) * r}

def residual(T, eps):
 """Heat absorbed by the products minus heat released, J."""
 n = moles(eps)
 heat = sum(n[g] * dH(g, T) for g in n)
 return heat - Q
```

[how] 
moles(eps) receives the excess of air as a fraction (0.2 for 20 %) and returns a dictionary with the moles of each gas in the products, per mole of CH4: the results of the mass balance.
residual(T, eps) receives a temperature and an excess. n[g] * dH(g, T) for g in n goes through the gases g in the dictionary n and produces, for each, moles times heat per mole; sum(…) adds them.
The function returns the heat absorbed minus Q. It is negative if T is too low (the products could still absorb more) and positive if T is too high.

[so-far] The program has a function whose zero, as a function of T, is the flame temperature.

[step] 

### Solving for the flame temperature

[purpose] Ask brentq for the temperature that zeroes the residual, for four amounts of excess air.

```
for pct in (0, 20, 100, 200):
 T = brentq(residual, T0, 5000, args=(pct / 100,))
 print(f"Excess {pct:3d} %: T = {T:.1f} K")
```

[how] brentq receives your function and searches for its zero. It calls residual many times, each time with a different trial temperature in the first argument, and uses the sign of what returns to shrink the interval. Its arguments:
[how] 
residual — the function whose zero is wanted. Do not write the parentheses: it is the function itself that is handed over, not a call.
T0, 5000 — the interval of the search, in K. The residual is negative at 298.15 K and positive at 5000 K, so a zero lies between them.
args=(pct / 100,) — the extra value that brentq passes to every call, after T. It is a tuple; the comma after the value is what makes it a tuple of one element.
[how] By default, brentq stops when the interval is narrower than about 10⁻¹² K (it has converged), far finer than the four digits we need. for pct in (0, 20, 100, 200): repeats the two indented lines once for each value of the tuple. In the f-string, {pct:3d} prints an integer (d) in a field 3 characters wide (3) and {T:.1f} a number with one decimal.

[so-far] The program prints the flame temperature for 0, 20, 100 and 200 % of excess air.

### Excess air for a target temperature, and a check

[purpose] Solve the third question directly, since the balance is linear in the excess, and confirm the result by an independent route.

```
T_target = 1500.0 # desired flame temperature, K
a = (dH("CO2", T_target) + 2 * dH("H2O", T_target)
 + 2 * r * dH("N2", T_target))
b = 2 * dH("O2", T_target) + 2 * r * dH("N2", T_target)
eps = (Q - a) / b
print(f"Excess air for {T_target:.0f} K: {100 * eps:.1f} %")

def cp(T, A, B, D):
 """Heat capacity of one gas at temperature T, J/(mol*K)."""
 return R * (A + B * T + D / T**2)

n = moles(eps)
heat = 0.0
for g in n:
 A, B, D = coef[g]
 heat += n[g] * quad(cp, T0, T_target, args=(A, B, D))[0]
print(f"Check, heat absorbed at {T_target:.0f} K: "
 f"{heat / 1000:.1f} kJ")
```

[how] 
a and b are the two pieces of the linear equation for eps at 1500 K: the heat taken by the part of the products that does not depend on the excess, and the heat taken per unit of excess. Then eps = (Q - a) / b.
cp(T, A, B, D) is the heat capacity itself, not its integral.
quad integrates a function numerically. It receives cp, the limits T0 and T_target, and args=(A, B, D), the extra values passed to cp after T. It returns a pair (value, error estimate); [0] takes the first element, the value, since the first element of a list or tuple has index 0.
heat += x adds x to heat. The loop does this for each gas.
[how] The check does not use the formula of dH: it integrates the heat capacity numerically, and the heat absorbed must equal the heat released, Q. The numerical integral stops when its estimated error is small enough, far below the 0.1 kJ printed.

[so-far] The program prints the excess air for 1500 K and shows that the products absorb exactly the heat released.

## Result

[answer] 

With 20 % excess air, the adiabatic flame temperature is 2066.4 K. With 100 % excess air it falls to 1486.4 K. A flame temperature of 1500 K requires 97.2 % excess air.

[figura; controles: Ttarget] 

### Why does the temperature fall as more air is fed?

The heat released is the same, 802.6 kJ per mol of CH4, but it is shared among more moles of gas. The flame temperature is 2311.2 K with no excess, 2066.4 K with 20 %, 1486.4 K with 100 % and 1140.3 K with 200 %.

### Where does the flame temperature reach the target?

The flame temperature crosses the target temperature of 1500 K at 97.2 % excess. At 100 % it is already 13.6 K below the target (1500 − 1486.4), which shows that the crossing is just before that point.

### Is the fall in temperature the same along the whole curve?

No. The first 20 % of excess lowers the flame temperature by 244.8 K (2311.2 − 2066.4), while the 100 points from 100 % to 200 % lower it by 346.1 K (1486.4 − 1140.3). The curve therefore flattens: the loss per percent of added air is much larger at the beginning than at the end.

### Take the target temperature to 2000 K

The target temperature line goes up and meets the flame temperature at only 26.4 % excess; at 1200 K it moves out to 177.3 %. Hotter targets need less air.
